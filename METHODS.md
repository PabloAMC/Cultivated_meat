# Cultivated meat model — methods (technical reference)

*This is the code-level reference: how each module implements the model, where every number comes from, and
how the code, the in-browser model and the prose are kept in sync. For a plain-language explanation of how
the model works, read “How the model works” at the bottom of the
[interactive explorer](https://pabloamc.github.io/Cultivated_meat/interactive.html); for the findings, read
[RESULTS.md](RESULTS.md). Every number lives once, in [`inputs.py`](inputs.py) (`python inputs.py` prints the
datasheet), and the cost equation exists once (`uncertainty.R_from_inputs`).*

## Design rules

1. **Every module runs on its own and produces its figure(s).** No module is a sketch.
2. **No module introduces a mechanism a later one deletes.** A module that needs a number it can't yet produce
   takes it as an input.
3. **Every number is sourced in `inputs.py`** with a provenance tag and, where uncertain, a Monte Carlo range,
   so the point estimate and the uncertainty band can't drift apart.

## Keeping code, the page and the prose in sync

The model exists in three coupled forms: the Python source of truth, the JavaScript port embedded in
`interactive.html` (generated by `build_interactive.py`), and prose that quotes numbers and describes
equations. Divergence between them is the main maintenance risk. Three layers of guard:

**1. Python ↔ JavaScript** (automated, `tests/run_parity.py`). Runs the page's JS under Node and checks it
matches Python to 1e-4 over ~2,000 grid points (price × both acceptance dials × elasticity × income), plus the
calibration solve, the milk check, the timing rung, the health dials, the entry-point price ratios, the weight
overrides and the importance breakdown. It also compares the page's Monte Carlo band with
`meat_market.monte_carlo` (US medians within 0.4 pp; different random generators, so only statistically) and
checks the two sample the same inputs. **Every band draws its inputs from one list per band type in
`inputs.py`** (`MC_COST_INPUTS`, `MC_DEMAND_INPUTS`, `MC_TIER_INPUTS`, `MC_TIMING_INPUTS`), injected into the
page's JS; they once sampled different sets and disagreed by ~1.4 pp. **Rule:** after any change to a model
function, run `python build_interactive.py` and `./run_tests.sh`. Never hand-edit the JS in
`interactive.html`.

**2. Code ↔ numbers in prose** (automated, `tests/test_golden.py`).
- Every model number quoted in the page's prose or tooltips is a `{{TOKEN}}`, computed at build time:
  shares in `build_interactive.illustrative_numbers()`, everything else (price ratios, $/kg, years,
  coefficients) in `build_interactive.derived_numbers()`. The test checks every token is computed, every
  computed token is used, and the built page contains each value. **Rule:** never hand-type a model number
  into the page; add a token.
- A tripwire (`check_derived_prose_numbers`) re-derives a few elasticities and the cost floor and checks the
  page quotes them.
- The Monte Carlo figures quoted in RESULTS.md and here (the commodity block and the regional table) are
  recomputed at the documented seed and N and checked against the text.
- External data (the Lusk [−3.4, −0.84] bracket, the tier offsets +0.2/−0.4/−1.5, citations) are sourced, not
  derived, and are guarded by review.

**3. Code ↔ equations described in words** (manual; no test can check English). When you change an equation
or its structure: search the methods block in `build_interactive.py` (`<details class="methods">`) for every
place that *describes* it, not just the equation; add or remove the prose when you add or remove a term; keep
`inputs.py`, `build_interactive.py`, METHODS.md and RESULTS.md aligned, preferring a pointer to the single
source over re-quoting a number; finish with `./run_tests.sh`.

## Sourcing discipline

The cost stack is anchored to **Pasitka et al. 2024** (*Nature Food*), the only cost study built on measured
production data, throughout. Scale-up risk is expressed inside Pasitka's own three reactor designs (their
Fig. 4), not through Humbird's headline $37/$51. **Humbird 2021** supplies only the physical feedstock
floor (amino acids, glucose), the reasons scale-up is hard (oxygen and CO₂ transfer, shear, sterility limits on
vessel size) and one cross-check of the plant cost, never a load-bearing cost. Company reports (medium at $0.20/L or less) are tagged **[GFI26]**: the GFI
2026 State of the Industry report describes them, and a GFI and MG Consulting amino-acid cost analysis
supports the level, but there is no peer-reviewed measurement at production scale, so they are treated as
directional evidence, not the central case.

## Where every number comes from

Every input falls into one of five categories; `python inputs.py` tags each.

1. **Sourced:** `p_conv`, `media_intensity`, `media_price`, `overhead` (Pasitka Fig. 4),
   `aa_intensity`/`aa_bulk_price` and `glucose_intensity` (Humbird), `glucose_price` (GFI & Hawkwood 2025), `eps_own` (scanner meta-analyses), `price_pb_mult` (GFI/NIQ),
   `taste_quality_p` (NECTAR), `w_eth` (Gallup), `pb_mainstream_frac`/`pb_share_target` (GFI/SPINS),
   `income_*` (World Bank, Muhammad/ERS), `price_wf_mult` (BLS), the Bass `p_innov`/`q_imit` (literature
   norms), and `neophobia_x0` (set to reproduce the ~5% cold share in Van Loo, Caputo & Lusk 2020).
2. **Derived** (computed, never typed): the price coefficient β (solved at cultivated's own price and share),
   the parity threshold (`p_conv − markup_add`), the cost floor, and the cost-path R endpoints.
3. **Solved to a target:** `w_realtissue_M`, `w_health_M`, `w_health_E`, to the 89% mainstream buyer split and
   the 1.2% plant-based share (published) and a ~6% mainstream meatless rate (assumed; no clean source, 0.3 pp of
   leverage). One equation per unknown: calibration, not fitting.
4. **Judgement, shown as a range:** `cult_sub_mult` (κ; bracketed by Lusk 2020), the acceptance dials
   `accept_x`/`theta_free_M`, long-run novelty `neophobia_x`, health image `health_x`, the tier ladder (scaled
   by `premium_resistance`), `markup_add` (bounded by the USDA farm-to-retail spread), the scaffold cost, and
   `loss_aversion` (symmetric by default).
5. **Assumed, with modest leverage** (the calibration re-solves around each): across their full slider
   ranges, the long-run share at today's price (8.7%) moves by 2.3 pp for `w_realtissue_E` (0–4), 1.7 pp for
   `w_taste` (1–10), 0.7 pp for `taste_quality_w` (beans' taste, 0.1–0.5), 0.3 pp for `wf_mainstream_target`
   and 0 for `w_slaughter_E`; for comparison, κ (3–6) moves it by 10 pp. The timing knobs (`accept_rate`,
   `p_innov`, `q_imit`) set *when*, not the ceiling. `other_nutrients_floor` (~$0.9 of the ~$7.7 floor: salts,
   buffer, vitamins) is a small pure assumption.

The single load-bearing **assumption** is `real_tissue_x = 1`: cultivated meat, being animal tissue, keeps
conventional meat's advantage with mainstream shoppers. It is exposed as a slider. The baseline also gives
cultivated a small **health edge** over conventional (`health_c = −0.1`, `health_x = 0`: no antibiotics or
contamination), worth about two points at parity (49% vs 47% without it); it is flagged, not relied on.

## The two outputs

- **Output 1: R = p_cult / p_conv**, and how close it gets to parity. Computed per meat type, because
  cultivated cost is about the same for every species while conventional prices are not.
- **Output 2: the market share** R buys, rolled up across meat types (by volume = impact, by value = market).
  Always a band.

Output 1 is a cost built from measured data over an observed price, and is the more trustworthy of the two.
Output 2 depends on how shoppers will treat a product nobody can buy yet.

## Step 1 — cost and the price ratio (`price_ratio.py`, `cost_model.py`)

```
media_cost   = media_intensity [L/kg] × efficiency × media_price [$/L]   (never below the feedstock floor)
biomass_cost = media_cost + overhead                                     (+ clean-room cost, if toggled)
p_cult       = biomass_cost + scaffold (structured cuts only) + markup_add
R            = p_cult / (p_conv × meat_tax)
parity (R=1) ⟺ biomass_cost ≤ p_conv − markup_add = $7/kg              (mince, $12 meat, $5 markup)
```

**Anchors (Pasitka):** `media_intensity` = 22.4 L/kg; `media_price` = $0.63/L (animal-free medium, down from
$3.31/L; removing albumin was the big cut); `efficiency` = 1.0 (their cells; 0.25 = CHO-like cells using
about a quarter of the medium). The default reproduces Pasitka's published **$24/kg** biomass cost.

**The markup is additive** (processing, cold chain and retail margin don't shrink as biomass gets cheaper). A
percentage markup would let the retail wedge vanish at low cost and make parity look easy. Its range floors at
$2, below conventional meat's ~$4, because cultivated skips slaughter and carcass breakdown; at that floor the
parity threshold rises to ~$10/kg of biomass.

**The scale-up lever (`overhead`).** Pasitka's costs are modelled for a 50,000 L facility at three reactor
designs; non-medium costs fall with vessel size because consumables and filters dominate small vessels:

| design | overhead | biomass cost | R |
|---|---|---|---|
| ATF, 0.5 m³ vessels: scale-up stalls | $24.7/kg | $38.8/kg | 3.65 |
| TFF, ten 5 m³ vessels: the default | $9.9/kg | $24/kg | 2.42 |
| perfusion, 20 m³: scale-up succeeds | $7.9/kg | $22/kg | 2.25 |

The Monte Carlo range for `overhead` is triangular 6 / 9.9 / 15: centred on the default design, with the
minimal-plant floor as the optimistic end. The small-vessel stall is a named downside scenario (in the
waterfall and the cost table), not part of the central band: a commercial plant would only fall back to many
small vessels if large reactors fail. Pasitka's continuous run was at 1.8 L, with pilot hardware at 300 L and
claimed scalability to 5,000 L.

**The floor (`cost_floor`)** is what remains when every reducible cost (recombinant proteins, single-use
filters, small-scale capital) is engineered away:

| component | value | basis |
|---|---|---|
| amino acids | ~$0.67/kg | 0.337 kg of soy hydrolysate per kg × $2/kg [Humbird Table 3.5]; it supplies the 0.26 kg of amino acids in Table 3.4 |
| glucose | ~$0.13/kg | 0.32 kg per kg at full respiration [Humbird Table 2.3, derived] × $0.40/kg [GFI & Hawkwood 2025] |
| salts, buffer, vitamins, trace elements | ~$0.9/kg | assumed (Humbird leaves them out as low-cost) |
| running a plant | ~$6/kg | minimal plant cost at scale [Pasitka: nutrients ~66–70% of perfusion costs] |
| **floor** | **~$7.7/kg** | R ≈ 1.06 |

*Glucose, physics first.* A cell uses glucose two ways. Some becomes biomass that isn't protein: lipid,
carbohydrate and nucleotides, 0.065 mol per mol of dry cell mass (Humbird's Reaction 2.6). The rest is burned to pay
the cell's metabolic power, which runs whether or not the cell grows. Per kilo of cells that cost scales with the
time a kilo takes to grow, 1/μ. Fully respired (no lactate) at Humbird's μ = 0.029/h, the total is 0.13 mol/mol, or
0.32 kg of glucose per kg of wet cells; faster growth would cut the respired half, toward 0.16 kg/kg. At $0.40/kg
that is ~$0.13/kg: glucose barely matters. The feedstock as a whole (~$1.7/kg) is close to a chicken's feed bill
(US broilers: ~4.6 kg of feed and ~$1.4 per kg of edible meat in 2025, ~$1.9 in the EU; from breeder targets,
USDA and Wageningen data in the pf-tea evidence, `data/evidence/feed.yaml`).

*What the floor assumes.* Humbird's own hydrolysate medium also buys free glutamine and tyrosine (~$2.6/kg); the
floor leaves them out, assuming cells that make their own glutamine (glutamine-synthetase lines, standard in CHO)
and tyrosine supplied by the hydrolysate. The plant term is Pasitka's: Humbird's independent estimate of the same
non-nutrient costs in his most favourable design is ~$14.5/kg (`humbird_plant_cost`, a cross-check, not used),
which would put the floor near $16/kg. The floor sits right at the parity threshold only on Pasitka's plant costs,
and it assumes Humbird's scale-up limits are engineered away; if they bind, it is out of reach at any medium price.

## Step 2 — demand (`market_share.py`)

A two-segment, four-product logit. Products: conventional `c`, plant-based `p`, cultivated `x`, whole food
`w` (beans, tofu, lentils). Attributes: price ratio, taste, slaughter-free (0/1), real tissue (0/1), health.
Segments: mainstream `M` (95%) and ethical `E` (5%, Gallup vegetarian + vegan).

```
V_sj = α·ln(y_eff − p_j)                                        # income-based price term (BLP)
       − λ·max(0, R_j − 1) + max(0, 1 − R_j)                    # reference-price term (λ = 1: symmetric)
       + w_taste·(a_j − 1) + w_slaughter[s]·g_j + w_realtissue[s]·b_j + w_health[s]·ζ_j
       + ν_j + τ_j                                              # novelty, authenticity: 0 unless set
P_sj    = softmax_j(V_sj)
share_j = w_eth·P_Ej + (1 − w_eth)·P_Mj
```

`p_j = R_j × p_ref`, where `p_ref` is the conventional price of the product being compared (each meat type
uses its own price). Every product uses the same rule; there is no cultivated-only term and no free fitted
constant (whole food's appeal is its health position, +2, times a solved weight).

**Why cultivated takes buyers from conventional meat, without a nest.** A single logit takes share from every
option in proportion. Here the shared real-tissue attribute makes conventional meat own almost all of the
large mainstream segment, so a proportional reduction there comes almost entirely out of conventional in
absolute terms. At parity, adding cultivated takes 44.4 pp from conventional and 0.56 pp from plant-based.

**The price coefficient.** In a logit, a product's own-price elasticity is `β · p · (1 − s)`. The target is
meat's measured elasticity, ε = −0.9 (Andreyeva 2010; Gallet 2010, 2012), times the closeness κ = 4: a single
cultivated product has a near-perfect substitute beside it, so it is more price-sensitive than meat as a
category. Price enters through two terms (the BLP log, slope β, and the reference term, slope −λ/p_c on the
premium side), so β is solved so their total hits the target at cultivated's own price and share (a short
fixed point, `market_share._derive_beta`):

```
β = κ·ε / (p_x·(1 − s_x)) + λ / p_c,      α = −β·(y_ref − p_x)
```

κ sets the level of price sensitivity; λ only shapes it around parity. At today's price the realised
elasticity is −3.6; once shoppers are familiar, it is about −0.8 at parity and −1.7 at R = 1.5. **Evidence for
κ:** Van Loo, Caputo & Lusk (2020) priced lab-grown meat at six levels; their models put the at-parity
elasticity between −0.84 and −3.4, and the model's implied value in that setting (equal price, first-contact
wariness) at κ = 4 is −1.5 (golden-guarded). No experiment has priced cultivated meat at 2.4 times
conventional, so the −3.6 there is an assumption (κ × ε), checked only against the data at parity.

The anchor price `p_x` is cultivated's price at the *default* costs, computed by the cost model: change the
defaults in `inputs.py` and it moves, but the page's cost sliders don't re-anchor it.

**The “seen as real meat” dials are what-ifs applied after calibration.** β is derived with cultivated counted
as real meat (`real_tissue_x = 1`, the premise), so moving `real_tissue_x` shifts the outcome, not the price
coefficient. The calibration always runs in the observed world, where plant-based is not real tissue, so
`real_tissue_p` is a counterfactual on top of it (re-fitting the weights at `real_tissue_p = 1` is degenerate).
Python and the page once differed on both; the parity test now checks them.

**Income (BLP).** `α·ln(y_eff − p)`: the same premium is a bigger bite of a smaller income. Raw BLP
(`y_eff = y`) makes poor shoppers about six times as price-sensitive as rich ones, too steep for food, so
income is damped: `y_eff = y_ref·(y / y_ref)^φ` with φ = 0.5, matching the roughly twofold gap in Muhammad et
al. (2011). US results don't depend on φ. The implementation is checked against its first-order linearisation
(within 0.01 pp at meat prices).

**Loss aversion (λ, default 1 = symmetric).** Up to Tversky and Kahneman's 2.25. Symmetric by default because
it barely moves the result once β is re-fitted (8.8% → 9.1% at today's price over λ = 1–2.25), cultivated data
can't identify it, and measured loss aversion largely reflects price-sensitivity differences between shoppers
(Bell & Lattin 2000) that κ already carries. It applies to every product by its own premium.

**Calibration.** Fixed from data: the plant-based premium (+77%, GFI/NIQ), taste deficit (0.8, NECTAR: only
~16% of products reach blind-taste parity), the ethical share (5%, Gallup), ε (scanner data). Solved at
runtime by three one-dimensional bisections (`solve_calibration`): `w_realtissue_M` (89% of plant-based buyers
are mainstream, GFI/Morning Consult), `w_health_M` (~6% of mainstream meals skip meat), `w_health_E` (the
ethical segment's residual plant-based rate), which together give plant-based its ~1.2% share (GFI/SPINS). The
solved mainstream health weight is 0.85, or 0.17× the taste weight, below the ~0.5× in willingness-to-pay
studies (Malone & Lusk 2017), so health is a minor lever for mainstream shoppers. `w_realtissue_M` is a reduced
form (real-tissue preference plus habit): habit can't be separated from preference without panel data
(Heckman), so it lives in the timing step instead.

**Self-checks** (`python market_share.py` prints them):

| check | result |
|---|---|
| [1] plant-based share, cultivated absent | 1.20% (target 1.2%); whole food 10.5% |
| [1b] plant-based buyers, mainstream / ethical | 89% / 11% (target 89%); mainstream meatless 6% |
| [2] cultivated at parity across the dials | taste 0.6 → 11.6%, 0.8 → 26.2%, 1.0 → 48.8%, 1.1 → 60.8%; “no slaughter” 0.5 → 59.4%, 1.0 → 68.5%, 1.5 → 75.5%; not seen as real meat → 10.2% |
| [3] where cultivated's share comes from (parity) | conventional −44.4 pp, plant-based −0.56 pp, whole food −3.8 pp |
| [3b] ethical segment's cultivated rate | 20% at parity, 9% at R = 1.6 |
| [4] plant-based milk, out of sample | 15% (observed ~15%; milk's positions are set by hand, so a weak test) |
| [4b] implied at-parity elasticity (κ = 4) | −1.54, inside Lusk's [−3.4, −0.84] |
| [5] plant-based at full price and taste parity | 10% (a prediction, not fitted) |
| [6] share at today's price, each judgement swept and re-solved | κ 3–6: 3.4% → 13.7%; everything else moves it by 0.5 pp or less |

**Scope.** A calibrated, partial-equilibrium discrete-choice model: parameters are calibrated to published
facts, not estimated, because there are no purchase data for cultivated meat yet. Deliberate simplifications:
two shopper types rather than a continuous distribution; one price coefficient; no supply side; habit in the
timing step; convenience only through the rollout.

## Step 3 — meat types and regions (`meat_market.py`), structured products (`scaffolding.py`)

Each meat type is run at its own conventional price (`p_ref`), with two tier adjustments to cultivated's
utility, both scaled by `premium_resistance` (ρ):

```
tier = basic (unstructured) | cut (structured, < 2.5× the species' cheapest form) | premium (≥ 2.5×)
τ_tier   = ρ · (+0.2 | −0.4 | −1.5)                      # authenticity offset (utils)
ε_tier   = (1 + ρ·(ψ_tier − 1)) · ε,  ψ = (1 | 0.8 | 0.3)   # pricier tiers are less price-sensitive
share_vol = Σ (ω_i / Σ ω_k) s_i;   share_val = Σ (p_i ω_i / Σ p_k ω_k) s_i
```

The listed volume weights sum to 1.00–1.025 by region (premium variants were added without trimming the rest
exactly), so both totals are normalised. Volume is weight of meat, not animals: a count of animals is dominated
by chickens, where cultivated does worst, and would be lower.

The tier values are judgement (no per-product authenticity data exist), so ρ scales the whole ladder and the
Monte Carlo samples it (0.5–1.5). What they produce (RESULTS §3): at today's cost, premium products have the
highest share of their category (cultivated is already cheaper there) but authenticity caps them near a
quarter; beef and seafood cuts displace the most volume; at the cost floor, cuts and ground beef overtake
premium; chicken and pork stay above parity even at the floor. Regional markets use local prices, consumption
mixes and income (`REGION_INCOME`); the low-income markets are rough. Biomass cost is the same for every species
(a per-type multiplier, 1 by default, is the hook for a species-specific cost study).

**Structured products (`scaffolding.py`):**

```
structured_cost = biomass_cost + scaffold_frac × material_price + process_cost
```

The material price is a judgement range ($2–20/kg; Gu et al. 2025 describe the materials but give no costs),
and the process cost ($1–15/kg) has no published cost study behind it: the most speculative numbers in the
model. At the central values they add $6/kg, the scaffold cost the page uses. A structured product competes
with $25–40/kg premium cuts rather than $12 commodity meat, which is why cultivated-seafood companies target
premium species: against sushi-grade salmon, R has a median of 0.85, with 83% of draws at or below parity.

## Step 3 — entry points (`foothold.py`, the page's chart 7)

Product-level reach. A luxury price splits into the price of the everyday (accessible) grade and a rent for
pedigree cultivated meat can't copy (Rosen's hedonic pricing), so R is taken against the accessible price. A
prestige core (a share χ of volume, 0.25 by default, from the wild salmon and bellota ibérico splits) never
switches. Cultivated's share of the rest comes from the same logit, with the mince or cut authenticity setting,
and displaced volume = share × (1 − χ) × volume. Cultivated is assumed to sell at cost (no margin).
`foothold.py` holds the product list, sourced price tiers and scoring; the per-product price ratio is
parity-tested against the page. The equations and limits are in the page's appendix A6.

## Step 4 — timing (`adoption_timing.py`)

```
share(t) = F(t) × ceiling(ν(t)),      ν(t) = ν_x + (ν_x0 − ν_x)·exp(−r·E(t))
```

`F(t)` is Bass diffusion (`p_innov` = 0.02, `q_imit` = 0.40): the fraction of the market the product has
reached. The standard Bass rates come from durable goods, and the familiarity fade below may partly overlap
with the word-of-mouth term, so timing is rougher than the ceilings. Novelty ν fades
from the cold start `neophobia_x0` = −2.8 (about 6% at parity, close to the ~5% in Van Loo, Caputo & Lusk
2020) toward the long-run `neophobia_x` (0 by default) as cumulative availability `E(t)` grows, at rate
`accept_rate` (mere exposure). The ceiling is the Step 2 share at the current novelty. Fading novelty cures
“it's new”, not “it's worse”: a taste deficit (`accept_x` < 1) persists, which is why plant-based meat stalls
on the same machinery. Neophobia is symmetric across the two novel meats (`neophobia_x`, `neophobia_p`).

The cold-start range (−3.5 to +1.5) spans the survey-framing band: the model gives ~6% at −2.8 and ~60% (the
warmest survey framing, Perdue 2024) at about +0.5; the upper end (+1.5, ~80%) extends beyond the surveys.

**Cost over time.** Rather than a smooth learning curve, the simulation is driven by named cost-milestone paths
(`COST_PATHS`): step functions where R drops when a milestone lands. The R endpoints come from the cost model;
only the milestone year is a declared unknown (`cost_paths_timing`).

## Uncertainty (`uncertainty.py`)

Not a time axis: in a pre-commercial field costs fall in milestone-gated jumps with unknown timing, so the
model gives the distribution of the endpoint instead. Monte Carlo (N = 20,000, seed 0) over
`MC_COST_INPUTS + MC_DEMAND_INPUTS` and `p_conv`:

```
basic product vs commodity meat:
  price ratio R:   P50 = 2.09   80% CI [1.63, 2.63]   0% of draws at or below parity
  long-run share:  P50 = 7.3%   80% CI [1.9, 22.4]
```

The median is below the default 2.42 because the efficiency range (0.25–1.0, mode 1.0) only allows
improvement. Pin any input from the command line (`--fix media_price=0.2`) to ask “if this lands, what then?”.

## Sensitivity (`sensitivity.py`)

Two views that can legitimately differ:

- **One at a time (the tornado):** each input swept across its range with the others at their modes. This is
  its potential swing.
- **Band width:** how much the Monte Carlo band narrows if the input is fixed at its mode (shared with
  `uncertainty.spread_contribution`). Not a variance decomposition: it doesn't sum to 100% and under-credits
  inputs whose mode is at the edge of their range.

For R: medium price leads both (swing 1.49, width 19%) because its range runs both ways; cell efficiency has a
large swing (0.88) but ~0% width (its range can only improve); plant cost owns 9% of the width but understates
reactor scale, whose worst case (the small-vessel stall) is kept out of the band. For share at today's price,
the cost levers lead, then long-run novelty, the value of “no slaughter”, taste, price elasticity and health
image. Figures: `sensitivity_tornado_R`, `sensitivity_tornado_share`.

## Cruxes

- **Cost (dominates):** does cost reach parity? Most likely not for the basic product (R median ≈ 2.1). The
  two unsettled levers are medium cost at scale (the top driver, two-sided) and reactor scale-up (least
  demonstrated, largest downside).
- **Demand at parity:** taste, acceptance as real meat and the value of “no slaughter” span ~10% (not accepted
  as real meat) to ~75% (strongly valued) at equal price. The model takes no stance.

## Sources

- **Pasitka, L. et al. (2024).** Empirical economic analysis shows cost-effective continuous manufacturing of
  cultivated chicken using animal-free medium. *Nature Food* 5, 693–702. doi:10.1038/s43016-024-01022-w. Cost
  anchors and the three reactor designs.
- **Humbird, D. (2021).** Scale-up economics for cultured meat. *Biotechnology and Bioengineering* 118,
  3239–3250. The feedstock floor and the physical scale-up limits.
- **GFI & Hawkwood Biotech (2025).** Driving down costs of fermentation-derived ingredients (doi 10.62468/trxj5734).
  US Midwest DE95 glucose price.
- **GFI (2026).** State of the Industry report: cultivated meat, seafood and ingredients. Company medium-cost
  reports ($0.20/L or less) and the GFI/MG Consulting amino-acid cost analysis.
- **Gu et al. (2025)**, scaffold materials. **Peacock (2023)**, EA Forum: displacement of meat by plant-based at
  parity.
- **Demand calibration:** NECTAR *Taste of the Industry* 2024 and 2025 (plant-based blind taste tests: ~16% of
  122 products reach parity); GFI/NIQ retail pricing 2024 (+77% plant-based premium); GFI/SPINS and Circana 2024
  (plant-based meat ~0.8% of meat dollars, ~1.7% of packaged meat; plant-based milk ~15%); Gallup 2023 (4%
  vegetarian, 1% vegan); GFI/Morning Consult 2024 (~89% of plant-based meat buyers are not vegetarian or vegan);
  Van Loo, Caputo & Lusk (2020), *Food Policy* 95, 101931 (lab-grown at equal price ~5%; its elasticity);
  Andreyeva, Long & Brownell (2010) and Gallet (2010, 2012) (meat elasticities); Malone & Lusk (2017)
  (attribute weights).
- **Income and regions:** Berry, Levinsohn & Pakes (1995), *Econometrica* (the price form); World Bank 2023–24
  (GDP per capita, PPP); Muhammad et al. (2011), USDA ERS (food price sensitivity by income); BLS 2025 (US dried
  bean price, `price_wf_mult`).
- **Timing:** Bass (1969); Sultan, Farley & Lehmann (1990) (typical Bass rates); Pliner & Hobden (1992) (food
  neophobia); GFI consumer research 2024 (the 5–60% framing range).
- **Price references and behaviour:** Tversky & Kahneman (1991, 1992); Hardie, Johnson & Fader (1993); Bell &
  Lattin (2000); Heckman (1981).
- **Entry points:** Rosen (1974) (hedonic prices); Bagwell & Bernheim (1996) (Veblen pricing); Wright (1936),
  Arrow (1962) and Spence (1981) (learning curves).
