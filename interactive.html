Cultivated meat: how much of the meat market can it win — and how fast? build 2026-09-26 10:48

Cultivated meat is real animal meat grown from cells, without raising or slaughtering an animal. This page is an open model of how much of the meat market it could take, and how quickly. Every assumption is a slider, so you can see how much the answer depends on it.

What the model says, at its default settings
  1. Cultivated meat would cost about 2.4× as much as everyday meat today ($29 vs $12 a kilo at retail, using the only measured production costs). Even if production costs fell to their physical floor, it would still cost about 1.04× as much: right at the edge of price parity.
  2. At the same price, it would win about 49% of the market, if people accept it as real meat. If they treat it like a veggie burger instead, it gets about 9%.
  3. At today's cost, that adds up to about 2.9% of the meat eaten worldwide (5.7% of the money spent on meat), and about 9.6% in Europe, where meat is dearest.
  4. Its best chances are in beef and seafood, the dearest meats. Chicken and pork are cheap enough that cultivated stays more expensive even at the cost floor. Luxury products such as foie gras and bluefin tuna can already be beaten on price, but their markets are tiny.
  5. These are long-run ceilings, not today's share. Starting from near zero, cultivated meat takes about 25 years to approach them, as it reaches shelves and stops feeling new (chart 5).

These are point estimates. The Monte Carlo button below turns the charts into uncertainty bands. The method, equations and sources are at the bottom of the page.

How the model works: method, equations and sources

How to read this section. The model is a chain of four steps (the strip at the top of the page). Each section below covers one step: what it does in plain words, the equation behind it, a worked example, and which chart shows it. Derivations, calibration details and answers to likely objections are in the technical appendix at the end, so you can skip them on a first read.

Numbers with a bracketed reference, like 22.4 L/kg [1], come from that source; judgement calls are labelled as such; every other number is computed by the model at its default settings. The symbols are listed with their sources in the parameter table (appendix A9).

Step 1. From cost to price

Everything downstream depends on one number: the price ratio \(R\), cultivated meat's retail price divided by the price of the conventional meat it sits next to. \(R=1\) means the same price; \(R=2\) means twice the price. The retail price is built up from three costs.

  • Growth medium. Cells grow in a nutrient liquid called the medium. Pasitka and colleagues measured that a kilo of cells uses about 22.4 litres of medium, at $0.63 per litre [1]. Several companies reported $0.20 per litre or less in 2025, and an analysis of amino-acid prices by GFI and MG Consulting supports that level [3], but there is no peer-reviewed measurement at production scale yet. Leaner cell lines could need fewer litres: the efficiency \(\eta\) is 1 for today's measured cells and 0.25 for cells four times leaner.
  • Plant running cost. Everything else needed to run the plant (reactors and other capital, labour, energy, consumables) adds \(h\) per kilo. It is set mainly by reactor scale, and it is the least demonstrated number in the model: Pasitka's three designs range from $24.7/kg (many small vessels) through $9.9/kg (the default) to $7.9/kg (two large perfusion reactors) [1]. Humbird explains why scaling up animal cells is hard: oxygen and CO₂ transfer, and keeping huge vessels sterile [2].
  • Getting it to the shelf. Processing, packaging, cold chain and retail margin add a markup \(m\) (default $5/kg, about the farm-to-retail spread of conventional meat). Structured cuts (a steak rather than mince) also need a scaffold, \(k\), which we set at $6/kg as a guess, because no published study has costed it [6].
\[ R \;=\; \frac{\overbrace{\iota\,\eta\,p_{\rm med}}^{\text{medium}} \;+\; \overbrace{h}^{\text{plant}} \;+\; \overbrace{k}^{\text{scaffold (cuts)}} \;+\; \overbrace{m}^{\text{markup}}}{p_c\,t} \tag{1} \]

Here \(\iota\) is litres of medium per kilo, \(p_{\rm med}\) the medium price, \(p_c\) the price of the conventional meat, and \(t\) an optional multiplier for a meat tax (1 by default).

Worked example (the defaults). Medium costs 22.4 × $0.63 ≈ $14/kg; adding the plant's $9.9 gives $24/kg of cells; adding the $5 markup gives $29/kg in the shop ($35 for a cut, with the scaffold). Next to everyday meat at $12/kg, \(R = 29/12 \approx 2.4\).

How low can it go? Cells must physically consume a fixed amount of amino acids and glucose (about $1.5 per kilo of cells), and even a very large, efficient plant has some running cost (about $6/kg). Together these set a floor of about $7.5/kg [2]. Price parity with $12 meat and a $5 markup needs cells at $12 − $5 = $7/kg or less. So the floor sits right at the parity line: \(R \approx 1.04\) even if every production cost reaches its floor. The model also stops the medium cost from falling below the cost of the feedstock dissolved in it, and a toggle adds Humbird's clean-room cost.

Uncertainty. Sampling the four cost inputs over their plausible ranges gives a median of \(R \approx 2.1\), with an 80% range of 1.6–2.6; almost no draws reach parity. The median is below 2.4 because the efficiency range only allows improvement on today's cells.

What moves it most: the medium price and the reactor scale (chart 3), then the markup. The meat price matters just as much, from the other side: a tax that raises conventional prices by 25% lowers \(R\) as much as cutting all of cultivated's costs by 20%.

Step 2. From price to a market share

Given a price, how many people would buy it? The model uses the standard economic model of choosing between products, a discrete-choice model [12].

Four options. For each meal, a shopper picks one of: conventional meat, plant-based meat, cultivated meat, or skipping meat for beans and other whole foods.

A score for each option. Each option gets a score (economists call it utility) that adds up what shoppers care about: its price, its taste, whether it is real meat, how healthy it seems, and whether an animal was slaughtered. Tastes vary from person to person and meal to meal, so the highest score doesn't win every time; each option gets a share that rises smoothly with its score. An option scoring one point more than another is chosen about 2.7 times as often.

Two kinds of shopper. About 95% of people are mainstream shoppers, who choose mainly on price, taste and whether it is real meat. About 5% are ethical shoppers (vegetarians and vegans [7]), who care strongly about slaughter and mostly eat beans and other whole foods. The market share is the population-weighted mix of the two.

Conventional meat's small health penalty stands for antibiotics and contamination; beans are “the healthy choice”. Beans' taste score is an assumption that doesn't affect the results, because the calibration re-fits around it.

The central premise. Cultivated meat is animal tissue, so, unlike a veggie burger, it keeps conventional meat's biggest advantage with mainstream shoppers. That is why, in the model, cultivated draws its buyers mostly from conventional meat, not from plant-based. The premise is a slider (“seen as real meat”): at equal price, full credit gives cultivated about 49% of the market, and no credit about 9%.

Tied to data, not guessed. The weights on each attribute are not free parameters:

  • The price weight is set so that demand responds to price the way meat does in grocery data (1% dearer, 0.9% fewer purchases [4]), made steeper for cultivated because an almost identical product sits next to it on the shelf (appendix A1).
  • Three weights are solved so the model reproduces three facts about plant-based meat: its ~1.2% share of the meat market, the fact that ~89% of its buyers are mainstream flexitarians rather than vegetarians [14], and a realistic ~6% of mainstream meals that skip meat by choice (appendix A4).
  • The taste weight sets the scale for all the others, since taste is the main reason people choose a food.

As an out-of-sample check, the same model with only the product's facts changed to plant-based milk (near price and taste parity in coffee, and no cheap alternative) predicts about 15%, milk's actual share (chart 6).

In symbols, the score of option \(j\) is

\[ V_j \;=\; \underbrace{\alpha\ln(y_{\rm eff}-p_j) \;-\; \lambda\,(d_j)^{+} + (d_j)^{-}}_{\text{price}} \;+\; \underbrace{w^{t}(a_j-1)}_{\text{taste}} \;+\; \underbrace{w^{rt}\,b_j}_{\text{real meat}} \;+\; \underbrace{w^{s}\,g_j}_{\text{no slaughter}} \;+\; \underbrace{w^{h}\,\zeta_j}_{\text{health}} \;+\; \underbrace{\nu_j+\tau_j}_{\text{novelty, authenticity}} \tag{2} \]

Price enters in two ways: through the dollars it takes out of the shopper's income (\(p_j = R_j\,p_c\) is the option's price and \(y_{\rm eff}\) an income measure, appendix A2), and through how much dearer or cheaper it is than the conventional meat beside it (\(d_j = R_j-1\); with the default \(\lambda=1\) this is simply \(-d_j\), and \(\lambda>1\) makes premiums hurt more than discounts help, appendix A3). \(a_j\) is taste (1 = as good as conventional); \(b_j\) and \(g_j\) are 1 if the option is real meat or slaughter-free; \(\zeta_j\) is how healthy it seems. The weights \(w\) say how much shoppers care about each attribute; the no-slaughter, real-meat and health weights differ between the two kinds of shopper. \(\nu_j\) (novelty) and \(\tau_j\) (authenticity) are zero unless you set them; they come in at Steps 3 and 4. The shares follow from the scores, first within each kind of shopper and then mixed:

\[ P_j = \frac{e^{V_j}}{\sum_k e^{V_k}}, \qquad \text{share}_j = w_{\rm eth}\,P^{\rm ethical}_j + (1-w_{\rm eth})\,P^{\rm mainstream}_j \tag{3} \]

What the model says at equal price. At \(R=1\), with cultivated tasting as good as conventional and counted as real meat, mainstream shoppers see two nearly identical options, which split that market. Cultivated gets about 49% and conventional about 44%. Cultivated comes out slightly ahead because it is also slaughter-free and has no contamination penalty; without that small health edge it would get about 47%. Two judgement calls move the result most, and no data can settle them until cultivated meat is on shelves:

if cultivated meat…its share at equal price

At today's price (\(R \approx 2.4\)) the same model gives cultivated about 9% in the long run (US, everyday meat): price is the binding constraint.

Step 3. Every kind of meat, every region

Cultivated meat costs about the same to grow whatever animal it copies, but conventional prices differ enormously: in the US, chicken mince costs about $5/kg, a beef steak about $20 and sushi-grade fish about $40. So the model repeats Steps 1 and 2 for each kind of meat at its own local price, then adds the results up.

Two ways to add up. Weighting each kind of meat by how much of it is eaten gives the share by volume: what matters for animals and climate. Weighting by money spent gives the share by value: what matters for the market. The two differ because expensive meats are a bigger slice of spending than of tonnage:

\[ \text{share}_{\rm vol} = \sum_i \omega_i\,s_i, \qquad \text{share}_{\rm val} = \sum_i \frac{p_i\,\omega_i}{\sum_k p_k\,\omega_k}\,s_i \tag{4} \]

where \(s_i\) is cultivated's share of meat type \(i\), \(\omega_i\) that type's share of all meat eaten, and \(p_i\) its price.

Mince, cuts and premium. Each species' products fall into three tiers: mince (ground and processed), cuts (steaks, fillets), and premium (a cut costing at least 2.5 times the species' cheapest form: wagyu, sushi-grade fish, heritage pork). Two things change as you go up:

  • Authenticity. Nobody misses “the real thing” in a nugget; for wagyu, the real breed and origin are the product. Cultivated gets a small bonus for mince (+0.2), a penalty for cuts (−0.4) and a large penalty for premium (−1.5).
  • Price sensitivity. Buyers of pricier meat react less to price: 0.8 times the usual sensitivity for cuts and 0.3 times for premium [5].

These tier values are judgement, with no direct data behind them. So a single premium resistance slider scales them all (0 = no tier effect, 1 = default, 2 = double), and the Monte Carlo samples it between 0.5 and 1.5.

What comes out (chart 1, at today's cost):

  • Beef and seafood are where cultivated can compete. Chicken and pork are cheap enough that cultivated stays more expensive even at the cost floor.
  • Premium products get the biggest share of their category: cultivated is already cheaper than wagyu or sushi-grade fish. But the authenticity penalty holds it to roughly a quarter of those markets, and the markets are small.
  • Beef and seafood cuts displace the most meat by volume. If costs fell to the floor, cuts and even ground beef would overtake premium, and the US total by volume would rise from 5.1% to about 27%.

Regions. Europe is easiest (9.6% by volume and 15.9% by value at today's cost), because its meat is the dearest and its shoppers are rich. The US comes next; China and the global average are pulled down by cheap chicken and pork. Low-income regions (India, Brazil, Nigeria) are hardest: meat is cheap there and shoppers are more sensitive to price (appendix A2). The model uses each region's own prices, mix of meats and income; prices and mixes for low-income regions are rough (appendix A7).

Species and families. For display, species are grouped into poultry and seafood; beef, pork, sheep/goat and rabbit stand alone. Cultivated's cost is the same for every species, because there is no species-specific cost study (a per-species multiplier, 1 by default, is where one would go). Fish cells, grown at lower temperatures, are the most likely exception once measured.

Step 4. Over time

The shares so far are long-run ceilings: where adoption lands once cultivated meat is on every shelf and no longer feels new. Two processes set how fast it gets there (chart 5):

  • Rollout. Availability spreads the way new products usually do: a few early adopters, then word of mouth (the Bass diffusion model [11], with standard rates).
  • Familiarity. New foods start at a disadvantage. In a US choice experiment, only about 5% of people chose lab-grown meat even at the same price as beef [8]. The model starts cultivated at about that level of wariness (\(\nu_{x0}=-2.8\), which gives 6% at equal price) and lets it fade as people keep seeing the product.
\[ \text{share}(t) = F(t)\times \text{ceiling}\big(\nu(t)\big), \qquad \nu(t) = \nu_x + (\nu_{x0}-\nu_x)\,e^{-r\,E(t)} \tag{5} \]

\(F(t)\) is the share of shelves reached, with Bass rates \(p_{\rm B}\) (early adopters) and \(q_{\rm B}\) (word of mouth). Novelty \(\nu\) fades from today's value \(\nu_{x0}\) toward its long-run value \(\nu_x\) (0, neutral, by default) as cumulative exposure \(E(t)\) grows, at speed \(r\). The ceiling is the Step 2 share at the current novelty.

Worked example (the defaults). At today's price, cultivated meat starts near 0%, reaches about 1% after 10 years and about 8% after 30, close to its long-run ceiling of about 9%. The curve flattens out around year 25.

The widest uncertainty on the demand side is how wary people are today. The same product polls anywhere from about 5% to about 60% depending on how the question is asked [10]: a cold, unbranded choice experiment gives 5%, while “cultivated chicken in a restaurant” gives 60%, which corresponds to \(\nu_{x0}\approx +0.5\). The slider runs from −3.5 (~3%) through 0 (no novelty effect, ~49%) to +1.5 (~80%), and the Monte Carlo samples it. It changes how fast cultivated meat reaches its ceiling, not the ceiling itself.

Plant-based meat runs on the same machinery, and its novelty fades too. It stalls anyway, because its taste gap and price premium don't fade.

Entry points: which products first (chart 7)

Charts 1 and 2 add up whole categories. Chart 7 looks at specific products, to ask where cultivated meat could launch first and how much conventional production each launch would displace.

A luxury price is mostly not cost. An A5 wagyu or a tin of caviar is expensive because of breed, origin and scarcity, a premium economists call economic rent. Cultivated meat can copy the meat but not the pedigree, so it can only compete with the product's everyday grade: farmed rather than wild salmon, crossbred “wagyu-style” beef rather than A5. The model therefore compares cultivated's cost with this accessible price, not the headline price. And a prestige core of buyers will pay for the genuine article at any price; that part of the market is out of reach (25% by default, the level in the only two published splits, for wild salmon and bellota ibérico ham).

For each product the model reports its price ratio against the accessible grade; whether cultivated is already cheaper, about equal, cheaper only at the cost floor, or never cheaper; the share it would win (the same shopper model as Step 2); and the conventional volume it would displace (share × the reachable volume). Cultivated is assumed to sell at cost, with no margin: the chart is about reach and impact, not profit.

The pattern. Where cultivated can already win on price (foie gras, bluefin tuna, sea urchin, wagyu, lobster), the markets are tiny: thousands to hundreds of thousands of tonnes a year. Where the volume is (beef steak, pork loin, chicken breast: tens of millions of tonnes each), it can't compete on price yet. That is how new technologies often spread: start where buyers pay a premium, then move down-market as costs fall with experience [18], [19]. Foie gras stands out as a first product: it is unstructured (no scaffold), expensive, and increasingly banned on welfare grounds that don't apply to a cultivated version.

What would change the conclusions

  • Upward: a peer-reviewed demonstration of animal-cell reactors of 20,000 litres or more at high density and sterility, which would bring plant cost toward the large-perfusion case; independent confirmation of medium below $0.30/L at scale; or a meat tax.
  • Downward: if reactors can't be scaled up (Humbird's CO₂ and sterility limits), production stays in small vessels, \(R\) stays around 3.65, and the floor is out of reach at any medium price.
  • Demand: whether shoppers accept cultivated meat as real meat, whether it tastes as good, and whether they come to value “no slaughter” together span 9% to 68% at equal price. None of this can be measured until it is on shelves, so the model leaves these as sliders rather than picking a side.

What the model leaves out

  • It is calibrated, not estimated. There are no purchase data for cultivated meat yet, so the demand side is a set of scenarios tied to related data, not a forecast.
  • Prices are taken as given: no supply response, competition or capacity limits.
  • Two kinds of shopper, not a full spread of tastes, and one price sensitivity per product.
  • Convenience, the third classic driver after price and taste, enters only through the rollout.
  • The mix of meats is fixed within a region; a meat tax scales all prices equally.
  • It models competition on today's price ladder. Many new technologies start in niches (pet food, new species, allergen-free or “no animal harmed” products) that this model scores as small.
  • Meat only. Cultivated egg and dairy proteins are made a different way (precision fermentation), and there is no comparable published cost study for them; they need a separate model.

Technical appendix

A1. Price sensitivity: where the price weight comes from

Grocery data give the price elasticity of meat as a category, \(\varepsilon \approx -0.9\) [4]: 1% dearer, 0.9% fewer purchases. Meat as a whole has no close substitute, so it is fairly insensitive. A single cultivated product does have one, conventional meat on the same shelf, so its own sensitivity is \(\kappa\) times larger: \(\varepsilon_x = \kappa\varepsilon \approx 4 \times (-0.9) = -3.6\) at today's price. The price weight \(\beta\) (the slope of the price score) is solved so the model delivers exactly that at cultivated's own price \(p_x\) and share \(s_x\), which depend on each other and are solved together:

\[ \beta=\frac{\kappa\,\varepsilon}{p_x\,(1-s_x)}+\frac{\lambda}{p_c},\qquad \alpha=-\beta\,(y_{\rm ref}-p_x) \tag{A1} \]

The \(\lambda/p_c\) term hands back the part of the price response already carried by the reference-price term in Eq. (2), so \(\kappa\) sets the level of sensitivity and \(\lambda\) only its shape around parity. There is no free “calibration price”: move a cost input and \(p_x\) moves with it.

Evidence for \(\kappa\). Van Loo, Caputo and Lusk [8] priced lab-grown meat at six levels. Their two models put its elasticity at equal price between −0.84 and −3.4. The model's implied value at \(\kappa=4\) is -1.5, inside that range. No experiment has priced cultivated meat at 2.4 times conventional, so the −3.6 there is an extrapolation along the model's curve. Along that curve the elasticity is about −0.8 at parity, −1.7 at \(R=1.5\) and −3.6 at today's \(R\): share falls slowly just above parity, then faster. \(\kappa\) is the most consequential demand number above parity: at today's price, \(\kappa=3\) gives about 14%, 4 about 9% and 5 about 6%. It plays the role that a nested logit's similarity parameter would play for a “real meat” nest.

A2. Income

The income part of the price score, \(\alpha\ln(y_{\rm eff}-p_j)\), is the Berry–Levinsohn–Pakes form [12]: the same premium is a bigger bite of a smaller income, so poorer shoppers are more sensitive to price. Taken literally, this makes poor shoppers about six times as price-sensitive as rich ones, which is too steep for food. So income is damped, \(y_{\rm eff}=y_{\rm ref}\,(y/y_{\rm ref})^{\phi}\), with \(\phi=0.5\) matching the roughly twofold gap in the data [15] (\(\phi=0\) removes income; US results don't depend on \(\phi\)). At For the same product at the same price as today, that gives about 9% in the US, 4% in China and 0.8% in Nigeria. Wealth relaxes price sensitivity but doesn't erase the other differences: even a very rich shopper buys cultivated only about 18% of the time at today's price.

A3. Loss aversion (symmetric by default)

People judge a price against the familiar price of the conventional product, and a premium can feel like a loss [13]. The term \(-\lambda(d_j)^{+}+(d_j)^{-}\) rewards a discount at rate 1 and penalises a premium at rate \(\lambda\). The default \(\lambda=1\) is symmetric; the slider goes up to Tversky and Kahneman's 2.25. It is left symmetric by default for three reasons: it barely moves the result once \(\beta\) is re-fitted (at today's price, 9% at \(\lambda=1\) vs 9% at 2.25); cultivated data can't identify it; and measured loss aversion largely reflects differences in price sensitivity between shoppers (Bell and Lattin, 2000), which \(\kappa\) already carries.

A4. Calibration, and the attribute weights

Fixed from data: plant-based meat's price premium (+77% [14]) and taste (0.8 [7]), and the ethical share (5% [7]). Solved: the mainstream real-meat weight and the two health weights, so that the model reproduces (i) the ~89% of plant-based buyers who are mainstream [14], (ii) ~6% of mainstream meals skipping meat by choice, and (iii) the ethical shoppers' small plant-based share. Together these give plant-based meat its observed ~1.2%. Moving the ethical share, \(\lambda\), \(\kappa\) or income re-solves them live, so plant-based stays anchored.

Only differences between scores matter in this kind of model, so one weight has to set the scale. Taste does (\(w^t = 5\)), anchored to willingness-to-pay studies that rank taste first (taste about twice health and three times safety; Malone and Lusk 2017). Each weight slider shows its size relative to taste. The solved mainstream health weight comes out at 0.17× taste, lighter than the ~0.5× in those studies. Beans' appeal is carried by their health score (+2) times a solved weight, rather than by a free constant; only the product of the two is pinned down by the data. Without it, plant-based meat would roughly triple, with mostly vegetarian buyers, contradicting the data.

A5. Three different “is it real?” questions, and the tier ladder
  • Real meat (\(b\)): is it animal tissue? Permanent, the same for every product, and the reason cultivated takes buyers from conventional meat.
  • Novelty (\(\nu\)): is it new, strange, safe? The same for a nugget and a steak, and it fades with familiarity (Step 4).
  • Authenticity (\(\tau\)): for this occasion, do I want the genuine article? Tier-specific and permanent: irrelevant for a nugget, decisive for wagyu.

They are separate because they behave differently: a cultivated nugget faces novelty but no authenticity demand; a cultivated wagyu faces both. A fourth attribute, health image (\(\zeta\)), is separate again: it is 0 by default because surveys find both a “clean meat” draw and an “ultra-processed” aversion, and it is sampled between −0.5 and +0.5.

The tier ladder in formulas, with premium resistance \(\rho\) scaling both the authenticity offset and the elasticity multiplier \(\psi\):

\[ \tau_{\rm tier}=\rho\,\tau^0_{\rm tier},\qquad \varepsilon_{\rm tier}=\big(1+\rho\,(\psi^0_{\rm tier}-1)\big)\,\varepsilon,\qquad (\tau^0,\psi^0)=\begin{cases}(+0.2,\ 1.0)&\text{mince}\\ (-0.4,\ 0.8)&\text{cut}\\ (-1.5,\ 0.3)&\text{premium}\end{cases} \tag{A5} \]

“Premium” is defined per species (at least 2.5 times the species' cheapest form), so every species can have one and a product doesn't change tier between regions. Wagyu and sushi-grade fish are well attested; organic chicken and heritage pork are real but smaller, so they carry little volume.

A6. Entry points: the equations behind chart 7

A product's headline price splits into the accessible price and the rent, and cultivated's price ratio is taken against the accessible price only (Rosen's hedonic pricing [16]):

\[ p^{\rm auth} = p^{\rm base} + \underbrace{(p^{\rm auth}-p^{\rm base})}_{\text{rent}},\qquad R = \frac{c_x}{p^{\rm base}},\qquad c_x = c_{\rm bio} + k\,[\text{cut}] + m \tag{A6a} \]

A prestige core, a share \(\chi\) of the category's volume \(Q\), pays the rent and never switches. Luxury sellers keep their price and give up volume rather than discount, the classic Veblen pattern [17]. Only the rest is reachable, and cultivated wins a share \(s\) of it from the same shopper model as Step 2, using the mince or cut authenticity setting (the premium tier is the prestige core, already removed):

\[ Q^{\rm reach} = (1-\chi)\,Q,\qquad s = S\big(R;\ \tau_{\rm mince\ or\ cut}\big),\qquad D = s\cdot Q^{\rm reach}\quad(\text{kt/yr displaced}) \tag{A6b} \]

Zones: about equal if the accessible price is within $3 of cultivated's cost; cheaper now if above it; only at the floor if above the floor cost; otherwise never on price. A single-grade product (no rent, \(\chi=0\)) reproduces chart 1's share exactly, so the two views agree. Authenticity is counted once: as removed volume for the prestige grade, and through the mince or cut setting for the accessible grade. Limits: no margin or profit is modelled, the shopper model is calibrated on meat (so it is indicative for luxury and seafood), and each product is treated in isolation. The accessible and headline prices are sourced per product (June 2026 retail and wholesale; hover a bubble for the basis); shark fin's price is an estimate.

The markup is added per kilo, not as a percentage. Much of it (processing, cold chain, retail handling) is genuinely per kilo, and conventional meat's farm-to-retail spread (USDA ERS, about $3–6/kg for ground beef) sets its size. But this is a modelling choice rather than a measurement, and a percentage markup would change the parity arithmetic. That is why the markup has its own slider.

A8. Questions a sceptic might ask

Isn't ~49% at equal price just assumed? It follows from the model's symmetry: two options that mainstream shoppers see as equivalent split that market. The sliders show how it moves if they are not equivalent (Step 2 table).

Why not a nested logit? The shared real-meat attribute and the two kinds of shopper already make cultivated take its share almost entirely from conventional meat, which is what a nest would do, with fewer unobservable parameters.

Is there really no fitted constant? Almost none. Beans' appeal is a labelled attribute (health) times a solved weight, rather than a free constant, but its position (+2) is assumed; only the product is identified (A4).

Isn't premium resistance just novelty again? No. Novelty is the same for every product and fades; authenticity depends on the tier, is permanent, and comes with lower price sensitivity (A5).

Why no habit term? Habit can't be separated from preference without panel data (Heckman [13]); in this model it lives in the slow rollout and fading novelty of Step 4.

Why is loss aversion symmetric by default? See A3.
