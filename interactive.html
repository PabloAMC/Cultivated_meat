What cultivated meat could cost, and what it would take

There is no single cost. How cheap a kilo of cells can get depends on assumptions that nobody has tested together. These include how little waste the cells make, how fast they grow, how the plant is built and how much gas the cells can stand. Each row below is one scenario. A scenario is a complete set of those assumptions, together with what has to go right for it. Costs are dollars per kilo of wet cells, in 2025 prices. “Share of world meat” is the long-run share by weight, once cultivated meat is on every shelf and no longer feels new. It is not a forecast of when that happens. The share is the median of 4,000 random draws, so half the draws gave less and half gave more. In each draw, the cost estimates and the demand dials (price sensitivity, acceptance and the retail markup) vary. The 10th to 90th percentile range is next to the median, and 80% of draws fall inside it. The share at exactly the cost shown appears in small type as a point estimate. Words used on this page. Medium is the nutrient liquid the cells grow in. Waste means the ammonia and lactate that cells give off as they grow. Waste slows growth, so fresh medium must wash it out. Perfusion means that fresh medium flows through the tank all the time. The cells are held back and harvested continuously. A column is a packed tower beside the tank. The culture runs down it in a thin film, and CO₂ leaves without bubbles. Gas the cells tolerate is the speed of gas rising through the tank, in millimetres a second. Bubbles and foam hurt cells, so the cells set how much gas can pass. A run lost is a batch ruined by contamination. It empties the tank and takes days to restart.

What the table says. Perfusion in a stirred tank is the cheapest combination. It costs $12.51 a kilo in the reference case and $29.92 in the central scenario. Nobody has run animal cells in perfusion above about 4 m³. The modes that have been run at 20 m³ cost more. In the reference case a fed-batch stirred tank costs $18.85 and fill-and-draw costs $18.46. In the central scenario they cost $42.87 and $46.21. Cells that make more waste limit a culture that is not washed out to a lower density. These costs assume 62 to 92 g of cells a litre. Nobody has shown that density for meat cells in a tank this size. The one 20 m³ plant for meat cells reports harvests of about 27 g a litre. A chemostat costs more than fill-and-draw ($34.75 in the reference case) because it must run below the cells' top growth rate. Batch costs the most of the stirred-tank modes ($65.35) because one fill of medium feeds only a thin culture.

An airlift costs about what a stirred tank costs when the culture is not washed out. In perfusion it costs more ($14.78 in the reference case) because its tall shape passes less gas per litre and clears less CO₂. A single-use bag needs a new bag every run. That makes it close to a stirred tank only in perfusion ($20.91), where a run lasts a month. Packed beds and hollow fibres exist only as small units, so they cost $3,726 and $610,039 a kilo in the reference case. Even a 20 m³ bed priced like a stirred tank, with carriers that cost nothing, would cost $42.19. The bed is seeded thin and emptied every run, so it makes a fraction of a perfusion tank's output.

  • Airlift (bubble column), chemostat: Not found with animal cells. Quorn runs fungus continuously in 155 m³ airlifts.
  • Airlift (bubble column), perfusion: Not found with animal cells at any size.
  • Single-use bag, batch: Single-use bags of 2,000 L are common in pharma, and 5,000 L bags are installed.
  • Single-use bag, fed-batch: Pharma runs fed-batch in single-use bags of 2,000 to 5,000 L.
  • Single-use bag, fill-and-draw: Repeated harvests have been shown in bench vessels. No 5,000 L bag run has been found.
  • Single-use bag, chemostat: Animal-cell chemostats have been run only at about 1 litre.
  • Single-use bag, perfusion: Perfusion runs in single-use bags of up to about 2,000 L. The densest cultures were in rocking bags of a few litres.

  • Packed bed, perfusion: iCELLis 500 beds can be perfused at this size, for vaccines. They have never been used for food cells.
  • Hollow fibres, batch: The Quantum grows cells for cell therapy at this size, about 6 g a run.
  • Hollow fibres, perfusion: The Quantum is perfused at this size, for cell therapy. No unit for kilograms of cells exists.
What limits each vessel
  • Stirred tank (20 m³ a unit): Oxygen and CO₂ move only through bubbles. Bubbles and foam hurt cells, so the gas flow is capped. A deeper tank passes less gas per litre and clears less CO₂. The stirrer must mix faster than the cells use oxygen.
  • Airlift (bubble column) (20 m³ a unit): The same bubble rules apply as in a stirred tank. The tank is taller, so the same gas flow passes less gas per litre and clears less CO₂. The gas mixes the culture, not a stirrer.
  • Single-use bag (5 m³ a unit): The physics is the same as in a stirred tank, at the bag's largest common size. The bag takes no pressure, so the pressure trick does not apply. The frame is cheap, but each run uses a new bag.
  • Packed bed (65 litres a unit): The carriers' surface per litre caps the density. Oxygen falls along the bed, which caps its height. The cells must be detached to harvest, so the bed is grown and emptied in cycles, and some cells stay behind.
  • Hollow fibres (480 mL a unit): The space between the fibres caps the density. Oxygen and food must cross the fibre wall. Cartridges are small, so a plant needs thousands. Cells are flushed out to harvest.

Perfusion assumes a hollow-fibre filter loop that holds the cells back. Its membranes cost about $0.37 per litre they filter. Batch and fed-batch run 2 doublings a cycle from a seed, plus 10 hours to clean and refill (Humbird's design). A lost cycle costs the cycle and 2 more days. The model prices each vessel at the largest unit built for animal cells, up to 20 m³. The evidence and sources are in research_notes/vessels_and_modes.md.

Tricks. These are ways to get past the limits below. The model sizes each one by calculation (see the research notes on gas exchange and on waste). Nobody has tried any of them with meat cells.

Feed or process. Cells that make fat from glucose give off CO₂ without using oxygen. This is most of why cells make 1.2 CO₂ per oxygen. Fat in the feed lowers the ratio to 0.97 and raises the CO₂ limit by a quarter (a carbon balance on Humbird's cell). Nobody has tested whether the cells stop making their own fat.

Feed or process. The standard tank's oxygen supply implies about 0.28 mmol/L, which is above air saturation. Pharma holds 30–50% of air saturation, and cells grow unimpaired at about 10–100%. A lower level leaves more room to push oxygen in.

Feed or process. The culture runs as a thin film down half a metre of steel packing, with no bubbles. The model calculates the size, the flow and the cost. Dense cultures have been pumped through fibres at up to about 15–24 tank volumes an hour for weeks, in volumes of 1–4 litres. Nobody has tested more than that. Culture that has lost CO₂ stays alkaline until it is back in the tank, so the more passes an hour, the more of the culture is alkaline at any moment.

A lower value strips more CO₂ per pass but makes the returning culture more alkaline. A value of 55 keeps the pH rise to 0.26.

Feed or process. More pressure dissolves more oxygen. It does nothing for CO₂. Steam-rated tanks take the pressure. Nobody has tested it in cells.

0.055 is the engineered cell, and only genetically engineered CHO cells have shown it. Bovine cells made 0.70, and 0.14 with glutamine replaced in the feed (feed or process, measured).

0.5 is the engineered cell's. Ordinary pharma cells make about 1.4, and cells adapted to it make 0.4 (selection or adaptation). Chicken cells make 1.05. A value near 0 needs knocked-out genes (genetic engineering).

Feed or process. The cell-free medium passes through a zeolite or resin before it returns to the tank. The sodium the sorbent gives back counts as salt. The model derives the capacity in medium and nobody has measured it.

What has and has not been measured

Has been measured

  • Perfusion at 100 to 214 million cells per millilitre at bench scale. The runs used CHO cells in a wave bag (Clincke 2013) and chicken cells (SuperMeat 2024, Pasitka 2024).
  • Ammonia made by bovine meat cells with and without glutamine (Hubalek 2023). With glutamine, the cells made 0.70 mol per kilo and doubled in 26 hours. Without it, they made under 0.14 mol and doubled in 43 to 54 hours.
  • Vow reports growing animal cells in a 20,000-litre food-grade stirred tank, drained and refilled, with no clean room.
  • A survey of makers found contamination losses of 11.2% of batches, or 19.5% beyond flasks. It did not state run lengths (Powell 2025).
  • Gas flows of 0.1 to 2 mm a second in tanks of 8 to 20 cubic metres with animal cells (Nienow 2006).

Has not been measured

  • Anything with dense meat cells in a tank above about 25 cubic metres.
  • Meat cells that make this little waste and still double in 24 to 30 hours.
  • CO₂ tolerance of muscle or fish cells. How much gas and foam dense cultures stand at 20 cubic metres.
  • A pumped column, extra pressure or recycled oxygen with meat cells.
  • A way to hold cells back at 20 cubic metres, and the real cost of a food-grade plant with shared medium.
  • Medium at $0.20 a litre and growth factors at scale, on a stated mass basis.

How the model works: method, equations and sources

Each step below has a one-line summary. Click it for the detail. The equations are in appendix A0. A number with a bracketed reference, like 30.5 L/kg [1], comes from that source. Judgement calls are labelled. The model computes everything else at its default settings.

Step 1 · Cost → price. The price is medium, plant running cost and retail markup, divided by the price of the meat it replaces. The result is 3.3× at the cost one company reports, 1.23× in the best case and 2.91× in the central scenario.

The price ratio R is cultivated meat's retail price divided by the price of the conventional meat beside it. A ratio of 1 means the same price, and 2 means twice the price. The retail price has three parts:

  • Medium is the nutrient liquid the cells grow in. One company, SuperMeat, reports 30.5 litres per kilo of cells at under $0.50 per litre [1]. Other companies reported $0.20/L or less in 2025, and an amino-acid cost analysis by GFI and MG Consulting supports that level [3]. Nobody has verified either at production scale. Leaner cells would use less medium. The cell-efficiency slider sets this, and 1 means as reported.
  • Plant running cost covers capital, labour, energy and consumables, and comes to $19.1/kg. This is the same company's running costs plus a yearly charge on its plant of 23.6% of the plant's cost. This rate is used for every case on this page [1, 2]. The company itself counts depreciation only, which gives about $5 less. The plant's cost depends on its class, on how dense the culture is and on how fast the cells grow (see “Cost scenarios” below). Scaling up animal cells is hard because of oxygen and CO₂ transfer and sterility [2].
  • Getting it to the shelf adds a $5/kg markup for processing, cold chain and retail. This is about conventional meat's farm-to-retail spread. Structured cuts add $6/kg of scaffold. That figure is a guess, because no study has costed it [6].

What the default cost is. The default cost is what one company reports for its own process, scaled up on paper. The model puts the cost of capital on the same footing as the other cases. The result is about $34 per kilo of cells [1]. It is the best-documented figure on a sound basis (100% cells and a realistic weight per cell). It is still a company's claim from 10-litre runs, not a peer-reviewed result. Its stated tank sizes and densities account for only about half of its stated output. Humbird's independent design for efficient cells comes to about $25–46 per kilo in 2025 prices [2]. Every cost on this page is per kilo of real cells, which are about 70% water and 20% protein, like meat. An earlier version of this page used a peer-reviewed study [20]. Its kilo turned out to be a wet pellet that is mostly liquid, so this page no longer uses it. The headline ratio uses a $12/kg benchmark for everyday meat (range $10–14). The charts of share by type of meat, share of all meat and which products first use each meat's local price.

Worked example. Medium costs 30.5 × $0.50 ≈ $15/kg. The plant adds $19.1, so cells cost $34/kg. The $5 markup makes the shop price $39/kg ($45 for a cut). Against $12 everyday meat, R ≈ 3.3.

Cost scenarios. Cells must eat a fixed amount of amino acids, glucose and salts, and that feed costs about $1.7/kg [2]. The salts, buffer and vitamins in that figure are an assumption. The plant must also be paid for. The plant's cost per kilo is its cost per cubic metre of tank divided by what a cubic metre makes in a year. The second part is physics: how much oxygen a cell needs, and how much oxygen and CO₂ a tank can exchange without bubbles killing the cells. The plant's cost per cubic metre comes from Humbird's design [2] in 2025 prices, item by item. His plant as drawn comes to $14.5/kg in 2018 prices, and most of that is a charge of 23.6% of the plant's cost a year. How these parts come out depends on ten decisions, which are the switches in “What decides it”. A scenario is a preset of them. Best case: every switch is at its cheapest setting that has a plausible route, and the cost is $9.7/kg. Central: feed and selection make the cells low-waste, the cells double every 36 hours, and the plant is food-grade. The cost is $29.9/kg ($26.8 in a food-grade plant with shared medium). Cautious: the cells are the best meat cells measured so far, and the cost is $43.8/kg. Parity with $12 meat and a $5 markup needs $7/kg. The reference. Earlier versions of this page called this number the floor. It is a standard perfusion tank with no tank tricks, engineered cells and no lost runs. The plant costs about $10.8/kg and the cells cost $12.5/kg. The page keeps it as a labelled reference, because it is the default of the model and the cross-checks against Humbird use it. It is not a limit, and the best case costs less. Financing. Humbird charges 23.6% of a plant's cost a year. This covers money at 7.5% repaid in 10 years, plus maintenance and insurance. That fits a first plant. His own report gives the food industry's cost of capital as 5.4% and uses a 20-year life for a yeast plant. Those figures make the yearly charge 17.3% [2]. The scenarios are long-run cases set against meat, a mature industry, so they use the lower rate. At the first-plant rate the reference would cost about $15/kg. The reported process and the cautious tier below keep the first-plant rate. How firm are they? Each scenario's switches stay fixed, but the estimates behind it can be off. These are the plant estimate, the retention device, the financing, the upkeep share of respiration, the medium price and the loss rate. With those uncertain, the central case costs $31.7/kg at the median (80% range $27.4–38.1). The best case costs $10.3 ($9.2–12.2) and the cautious case $46.2 ($39.2–54.5). When demand varies as well (price sensitivity, acceptance and the retail markup), the long-run share of world meat is 0.2% in the central case at the median (80% range 0.1–0.6%). It is 3.8% in the best case (1.2–9.7%) and 0.1% in the cautious case (0.0–0.2%). The draws do not vary what the cells are like, how fast they grow or the other switches. The scenarios differ in exactly those things, and the chart “What to prioritise” shows what each is worth. A plant as cheap as a yeast plant is not used in any scenario. Plants for yeast and other microbes cost about a quarter as much per cubic metre as Humbird's design for animal cells. That would cut the plant term to about $6.3/kg (cells $8.0/kg in the reference tank). But animal cells are far easier to contaminate, and nobody has shown that they can grow in such a plant. For comparison only, the same reference case in a pharma-style plant with clean rooms, tanks simply drained and refilled, and first-plant financing costs about $19/kg.

Uncertainty. Across the plausible ranges of the four cost inputs, the median is R ≈ 3.3 (80% range 2.6–4.2). Almost no draws reach parity. The median is below 3.3 because cell efficiency can only improve on the reported process.

What changes it most. The medium price and the reactor scale matter most (see the cost-against-medium-price chart), then the markup. A 25% meat tax lowers R as much as a 20% cut in every cost.

Step 2 · Price → choice. Shoppers choose among four options. They weigh price, taste, health, “is it real meat?” and “no slaughter”. At equal price, cultivated meat gets ~49% if shoppers see it as real meat.

This is a standard discrete-choice model [12]. For each meal, a shopper picks conventional meat, plant-based meat, cultivated meat, or beans and other whole foods. The shares therefore include the bean meals. Each option gets a score that adds up its price, taste, health, whether it is real meat and whether an animal was killed. Tastes vary, so the top score does not always win. Each option's share rises smoothly with its score, and one more point means about 2.7 times as often.

Two kinds of shopper. 95% are mainstream, and they choose mainly on price, taste and real meat. 5% are vegetarian or vegan [7]. They care about slaughter and mostly eat beans.

Tied to data. Price sensitivity follows grocery data for meat (1% dearer, 0.9% fewer purchases [4]). It is steeper for cultivated meat because an almost identical product is next to it (A1). Three weights are solved so that the model reproduces plant-based meat's ~1.2% share and ~89% mainstream buyers [14], plus an assumed ~6% of mainstream meals that skip meat (A4). The model reproduces them to within a factor of about 2.6 at the default price sensitivity (κ = 8.5). It over-predicted them sixfold at its earlier default of 4. For example, it gives about 19% for a real-meat product at 1.44× the price of chicken cuts, and about 1% at 2.6× mince. It still over-predicts at small premiums and under-predicts at large ones. Real premium buyers look like a small group that pays whatever the premium, and one price-sensitivity number cannot capture that.

Step 3 · Every meat, every region. The model repeats the choice at each meat's local price and adds up the results. The total is ~0.3% of world meat by weight (1.0% by value) and ~1.3% in Europe.

Cultivated meat costs about the same whatever animal it copies, but conventional prices vary. In the US, chicken mince costs about $5/kg, a beef steak $20 and sushi-grade fish $40. So the model runs each meat at its local price and adds up the results by weight (closest to the climate footprint) and by value (the market). Neither total counts animals. Most land animals raised for meat are chickens, and cultivated meat does worst there.

Mince, cuts and premium. Premium means a cut at least 2.5 times the species' cheapest form (for example wagyu and sushi-grade fish). Higher up the ladder, authenticity matters more. Nobody misses “the real thing” in a nugget, but for wagyu the breed is the product. The offsets are +0.2 for mince, −0.4 for cuts and −1.5 for premium. Buyers also react less to price [5], at 0.8× for cuts and 0.3× for premium. The directions are documented. The values are judgement, scaled by one premium resistance slider that the Monte Carlo samples (0.5–1.5).

What comes out (share by type of meat, at the default cost):

  • Beef and seafood are where cultivated meat can compete. Chicken and pork stay dearer even in the best case.
  • Premium products get the biggest share of their category, because cultivated meat is already cheaper there. Authenticity limits that share to about a quarter, and the markets are small.
  • Beef and seafood cuts displace the most meat. In the central scenario, cuts and ground beef overtake premium. The US total then rises from 0.6% (at the reported cost) to ~1% by weight.

Regions. Europe is easiest (1.3% by weight, 3.2% by value), because meat is dear and shoppers are rich. Cheap chicken and pork lower the figures for China and for the world average. Low-income regions are hardest, because meat is cheap and shoppers are price-sensitive (A2). The prices and mixes for low-income regions are rough (A7).

Step 4 · Over time. The share starts near zero. It rises as the product spreads and stops feeling new, and it levels off after about 30 years.
  • Rollout. Availability spreads like it does for other new products, first among early adopters and then by word of mouth (Bass diffusion [11], with standard rates from durable goods).
  • Familiarity. In a US choice experiment, only ~5% chose lab-grown meat at the same price as beef [8]. The model starts about there (~6% at equal price) and lets the wariness fade with exposure.

Worked example (US, everyday meat, with the default price held fixed). The share is near 0% at launch, ~0% after 10 years and ~0% after 30. The long-run ceiling is ~0%. The curve flattens around year 30.

For timing, the biggest unknown is how wary people are at the start. Surveys range from ~5% (a cold choice experiment) to ~60% (“cultivated chicken in a restaurant”) [10]. The “starting wariness” slider spans that range (~3% at −3.5, ~49% at 0, ~60% near +0.5, ~80% at +1.5). It changes how fast the share grows, not how far. Plant-based meat follows the same rules but stalls, because its taste and price gaps do not fade.

Which products first. Cultivated meat can already beat luxury products on price, but those markets are tiny. Commodity meat is out of reach, even in the best case.

A luxury price is mostly not cost. A5 wagyu or caviar is dear because of breed, origin and scarcity (economic rent). Cultivated meat can copy the meat but not the pedigree. So the model compares its cost with the product's everyday grade (farmed salmon, crossbred wagyu). A prestige core of buyers never switches. The default is 25%, a proxy taken from the only two published splits (wild salmon and bellota ibérico).

Cultivated meat is already cheaper than foie gras, bluefin tuna, sea urchin and wagyu, and lobster is close. Each of these markets is only thousands to hundreds of thousands of tonnes a year. Commodity beef, pork and chicken are tens of millions of tonnes each, and the which-products-first chart uses whole-category volumes. Cultivated meat stays out of reach for these at world prices. New technologies often start at the top and move down-market as costs fall [18], [19]. Foie gras stands out. It is unstructured, it is expensive, and more places are banning it on welfare grounds that do not apply to a cultivated version.

The which-products-first chart gives luxury products higher shares than the share-by-type-of-meat chart does. It removes the prestige core and treats the rest like ordinary cuts. The share-by-type-of-meat chart applies one large penalty to the whole category, and that is the conservative view. The which-products-first chart assumes sale at cost, so it shows reach and impact, not profit.

What would change the conclusions. Cheaper classes of plant, better cells or cheap medium at scale would raise the share. Low density at scale would lower it. How shoppers react could change it either way.
  • Up: animal cells grown in plants built like microbial fermentation plants, efficient cells shown at pilot scale with a full mass balance, perfusion with a scalable way to hold cells back at 20,000 litres, independently confirmed medium below $0.30/L at scale, or a meat tax.
  • Down: if cells reach only half the density in a pharma-style plant, R stays around 4.52. Contamination losses in long runs would also lower the share, and the model does not count them.
  • Demand: acceptance as real meat, taste and the value of “no slaughter” together span 10%–68% at equal price. Nobody can measure these before launch, so they stay sliders.
What the model leaves out. The model leaves out supply and competition, a full spread of tastes, niche entry routes, and eggs and dairy.
  • Calibrated, not estimated. No purchase data exist yet, so demand is a set of scenarios, not a forecast.
  • Prices are given. The model has no supply response, competition or capacity limits.
  • Two kinds of shopper and one price sensitivity per product. Convenience enters only through the rollout.
  • Timing is rougher than the ceilings. It uses a durable-goods Bass curve plus a familiarity fade, and the two may overlap. Costs stay fixed.
  • A fixed mix of meats in each region. A meat tax scales all prices equally.
  • The existing price ladder. Niche routes (pet food, new species and “no animal harmed” products) score as small.
  • Meat only. Cultivated egg and dairy proteins use precision fermentation and need their own model.

A0. The equations

Step 1, the price ratio.

\[ R \;=\; \frac{\overbrace{\iota\,\eta\,p_{\rm med}}^{\text{medium}} \;+\; \overbrace{h}^{\text{plant}} \;+\; \overbrace{k}^{\text{scaffold (cuts)}} \;+\; \overbrace{m}^{\text{markup}}}{p_c\,t} \tag{1} \]

\(\iota\): litres of medium per kilo; \(\eta\): cell efficiency (1 = as reported); \(p_{\rm med}\): medium price; \(h\): plant running cost; \(k\): scaffold (cuts only); \(m\): markup; \(p_c\): conventional price; \(t\): meat-tax multiplier. The medium cost never falls below the feedstock dissolved in it.

Step 2, the score and the shares.

\[ V_j \;=\; \underbrace{\alpha\ln(y_{\rm eff}-p_j) \;-\; \lambda\,(d_j)^{+} + (d_j)^{-}}_{\text{price}} \;+\; \underbrace{w^{t}(a_j-1)}_{\text{taste}} \;+\; \underbrace{w^{rt}\,b_j}_{\text{real meat}} \;+\; \underbrace{w^{s}\,g_j}_{\text{no slaughter}} \;+\; \underbrace{w^{h}\,\zeta_j}_{\text{health}} \;+\; \underbrace{\nu_j+\tau_j}_{\text{novelty, authenticity}} \tag{2} \] \[ P_j = \frac{e^{V_j}}{\sum_k e^{V_k}}, \qquad \text{share}_j = w_{\rm eth}\,P^{\rm ethical}_j + (1-w_{\rm eth})\,P^{\rm mainstream}_j \tag{3} \]

Price enters twice: through the dollars it takes from income (\(p_j = R_j\,p_c\); \(y_{\rm eff}\) is an income measure, A2), and through the premium over conventional meat, \(d_j = R_j-1\), split into its positive part \((d_j)^{+}\) and negative part \((d_j)^{-}\) (at the default \(\lambda=1\) the two terms are simply \(-d_j\); A3). \(a_j\): taste (1 = as good as conventional); \(b_j\), \(g_j\): 1 if real meat, 1 if slaughter-free; \(\zeta_j\): health image. The weights \(w\) (A4) differ between the two kinds of shopper for no slaughter, real meat and health. \(\nu_j\) (novelty) and \(\tau_j\) (authenticity by tier, A5) are zero unless set.

Step 3, adding up.

\[ \text{share}_{\rm vol} = \sum_i \frac{\omega_i}{\sum_k \omega_k}\,s_i, \qquad \text{share}_{\rm val} = \sum_i \frac{p_i\,\omega_i}{\sum_k p_k\,\omega_k}\,s_i \tag{4} \]

\(s_i\): cultivated's share of meat type \(i\); \(\omega_i\): that type's share of meat eaten (the listed weights sum to slightly more than 1 in some regions, hence the normalisation); \(p_i\): its local price.

Step 4, over time.

\[ \text{share}(t) = F(t)\times \text{ceiling}\big(\nu(t)\big), \qquad \nu(t) = \nu_x + (\nu_{x0}-\nu_x)\,e^{-r\,E(t)} \tag{5} \]

\(F(t)\): the fraction of the market reached, a Bass curve with rates \(p_{\rm B}\) (early adopters) and \(q_{\rm B}\) (word of mouth). Novelty \(\nu\) fades from its starting value \(\nu_{x0}\) toward its long-run value \(\nu_x\) as cumulative exposure \(E(t)\) grows, at speed \(r\). The ceiling is the Step 2 share at that novelty.

A1. Price sensitivity: where the price weight comes from

Grocery data give the price elasticity of meat as a category, \(\varepsilon \approx -0.9\) [4]. This means that meat 1% dearer sells 0.9% less. Meat as a whole has no close substitute, so it is fairly insensitive to price. A single cultivated product does have a close substitute, which is conventional meat on the same shelf. So a single product is more sensitive than the category. At the calibration price the sensitivity is \(4 \times (-0.9) = -3.6\), the usual gap between a product and its category. That sets the price weight that every product shares. Cultivated meat's price difference from the conventional meat beside it then counts \(\kappa/4\) times as much again, because the two are near-identical (\(\kappa = 8.5\) by default). The model solves the price weight \(\beta\) (the slope of the price score) so that it delivers exactly that sensitivity at the calibration price \(p_x\) and at cultivated meat's share there, \(s_x\). These two depend on each other, so the model solves them together:

\[ \beta=\frac{\kappa\,\varepsilon}{p_x\,(1-s_x)}+\frac{\lambda}{p_c},\qquad \alpha=-\beta\,(y_{\rm ref}-p_x) \tag{A1} \]

The \(\lambda/p_c\) term adds back the part of the price response that the reference-price term in Eq. (2) already contains. So \(\kappa\) sets the level of sensitivity, and \(\lambda\) sets only its shape around parity. The price \(p_x\) is a fixed calibration price of $29/kg. It is cultivated meat's shop price before the model added the plant's capital charge to the cost. The model holds it fixed, so that correcting a cost changes cultivated meat's price but not how shoppers respond to price. At the reported cost, the shop price is higher ($39/kg), and the model is more price-sensitive there.

Evidence for \(\kappa\). There are two kinds. First, Van Loo, Caputo and Lusk [8] priced lab-grown meat at six levels. Their two models put its elasticity at equal price between −0.84 and −3.4. The model's implied value in the same setting (equal price, first-contact wariness) is -3.3, which is inside that range. At \(\kappa = 9\) it would be just outside. Second, premium animal products show what share a product takes when it is the conventional one plus a virtue, at a higher price. Organic milk, cage-free, organic and omega-3 eggs, organic chicken and organic meat take 2–7% of volume at 1.5 to 2.2 times the conventional price. At \(\kappa = 4\), this model's default until November 2026, the model over-predicts those six about sixfold. At 8.5 the typical miss is under threefold, and the best fit is at 10. So 8.5 is the best fit to the premium products within what the experiment allows. \(\kappa\) changes nothing at equal price. Once shoppers are familiar with the product, the model's elasticity is about −1.7 at parity, −4.4 at \(R=1.5\) and −8.3 at \(R=2.4\). At the reported cost, \(\kappa=4\) gives about 2.3%, 8.5 about 0.0% and 10 about 0.0%. \(\kappa\) plays the role that a nested logit's similarity parameter would play for a “real meat” nest, but it applies to price only.

A2. Income

The income part of the price score, \(\alpha\ln(y_{\rm eff}-p_j)\), has the Berry–Levinsohn–Pakes form [12]. The same premium is a bigger bite of a smaller income, so poorer shoppers are more sensitive to price. Taken literally, this makes poor shoppers about six times as price-sensitive as rich ones, which is too steep for food. So the model damps income, \(y_{\rm eff}=y_{\rm ref}\,(y/y_{\rm ref})^{\phi}\), with \(\phi=0.5\) matching the roughly twofold gap in the data [15]. (\(\phi=0\) removes income. US results do not depend on \(\phi\).) For the same product at the default price, this gives about 0% in the US, 0% in China and 0.0% in Nigeria. Wealth relaxes price sensitivity but does not erase the other differences. Even a very rich shopper buys cultivated meat only about 1% of the time at the default price.

A3. Loss aversion (symmetric by default)

People judge a price against the familiar price of the conventional product, and a premium can feel like a loss [13]. The term \(-\lambda(d_j)^{+}+(d_j)^{-}\) rewards a discount at rate 1 and penalises a premium at rate \(\lambda\). The default \(\lambda=1\) is symmetric, and the slider goes up to Tversky and Kahneman's 2.25. The model leaves it symmetric by default for three reasons. First, it barely changes the result once \(\beta\) is re-fitted (at the default price, 0% at \(\lambda=1\) against 0% at 2.25). Second, cultivated meat data cannot identify it. Third, measured loss aversion largely reflects differences in price sensitivity between shoppers (Bell and Lattin, 2000), and \(\kappa\) already contains those.

A4. Calibration, and the attribute weights

Fixed from data: plant-based meat's price premium (+77% [14]) and taste (0.8 [7]), and the ethical share (5% [7]). Solved: the mainstream real-meat weight and the two health weights, so that the model reproduces (i) the ~89% of plant-based buyers who are mainstream [14], (ii) ~6% of mainstream meals skipping meat by choice, and (iii) the ethical shoppers' small plant-based share. Together these give plant-based meat its observed ~1.2%. Moving the ethical share, \(\lambda\), \(\kappa\) or income re-solves them live, so plant-based stays anchored.

Only differences between scores matter in this kind of model, so one weight has to set the scale. Taste sets it (\(w^t = 5\)). This is anchored to willingness-to-pay studies that rank taste first (taste about twice health and three times safety, Malone and Lusk 2017). Each weight slider shows its size relative to taste. The solved mainstream health weight comes out at 0.17× taste, which is lighter than the ~0.5× in those studies. Beans' appeal comes from their health score (+2) times a solved weight, not from a free constant. The data pin down only the product of the two. Without this, plant-based meat would roughly triple, with mostly vegetarian buyers, which contradicts the data.

A5. Three different “is it real?” questions, and the tier ladder
  • Real meat (\(b\)) asks whether it is animal tissue. The answer is permanent and the same for every product, and it is the reason cultivated meat takes buyers from conventional meat.
  • Novelty (\(\nu\)) asks whether it is new, strange or safe. It is the same for a nugget and a steak, and it fades with familiarity (Step 4).
  • Authenticity (\(\tau\)) asks whether, for this occasion, the shopper wants the genuine article. It depends on the tier and is permanent. It is irrelevant for a nugget and decisive for wagyu.

They are separate because they behave differently. A cultivated nugget faces novelty but no authenticity demand. A cultivated wagyu faces both. A fourth attribute, health image (\(\zeta\)), is separate again. It is 0 by default, because surveys find both a “clean meat” appeal and an “ultra-processed” aversion. The Monte Carlo samples it between −0.5 and +0.5.

The tier ladder in formulas, with premium resistance \(\rho\) scaling both the authenticity offset and the elasticity multiplier \(\psi\):

\[ \tau_{\rm tier}=\rho\,\tau^0_{\rm tier},\qquad \varepsilon_{\rm tier}=\big(1+\rho\,(\psi^0_{\rm tier}-1)\big)\,\varepsilon,\qquad (\tau^0,\psi^0)=\begin{cases}(+0.2,\ 1.0)&\text{mince}\\ (-0.4,\ 0.8)&\text{cut}\\ (-1.5,\ 0.3)&\text{premium}\end{cases} \tag{A5} \]

The model defines “premium” per species (at least 2.5 times the species' cheapest form). So every species can have a premium tier, and a product does not change tier between regions. Wagyu and sushi-grade fish are well attested. Organic chicken and heritage pork are real but smaller, so they have little volume.

A6. Entry points: the equations behind the which-products-first chart

A product's headline price splits into the accessible price and the rent. The model takes cultivated meat's price ratio against the accessible price only (Rosen's hedonic pricing [16]):

\[ p^{\rm auth} = p^{\rm base} + \underbrace{(p^{\rm auth}-p^{\rm base})}_{\text{rent}},\qquad R = \frac{c_x}{p^{\rm base}},\qquad c_x = c_{\rm bio} + k\,[\text{cut}] + m \tag{A6a} \]

A prestige core, a share \(\chi\) of the category's volume \(Q\), pays the rent and never switches. Luxury sellers keep their price and give up volume rather than discount, which is the classic Veblen pattern [17]. Only the rest is reachable. Cultivated meat wins a share \(s\) of it from the same shopper model as Step 2, using the mince or cut authenticity setting. (The premium tier is the prestige core, which the model has already removed.)

\[ Q^{\rm reach} = (1-\chi)\,Q,\qquad s = S\big(R;\ \tau_{\rm mince\ or\ cut}\big),\qquad D = s\cdot Q^{\rm reach}\quad(\text{kt/yr displaced}) \tag{A6b} \]

Zones. A product is about equal if the accessible price is within $3 of cultivated meat's cost. It is cheaper now if the accessible price is above that. It is only in the best case if the accessible price is above the best case's cost. Otherwise it is never on price. A single-grade product (no rent, \(\chi=0\)) reproduces the share-by-type-of-meat chart exactly, so the two views agree. The model counts authenticity once, as removed volume for the prestige grade and through the mince or cut setting for the accessible grade. The model has limits. It does not model margin or profit, its shopper model is calibrated on meat (so it is indicative for luxury and seafood), and it treats each product in isolation. The accessible and headline prices come from sources for each product (June 2026 retail and wholesale, with the basis shown when you hover over a bubble). Shark fin's price is an estimate.

A8. Questions a sceptic might ask

Isn't ~49% at equal price just assumed? It follows from the model's symmetry. Two options that mainstream shoppers see as equivalent split that market. The sliders show how the share changes if they are not equivalent (Step 2 table).

Why not a nested logit? The shared real-meat attribute and the two kinds of shopper already make cultivated take its share almost entirely from conventional meat, which is what a nest would do, with fewer unobservable parameters.

Is there really no fitted constant? Almost none. Beans' appeal is a labelled attribute (health) times a solved weight, not a free constant. The model assumes the attribute's position (+2), so only the product of the two is identified (A4).

Isn't premium resistance just novelty again? No. Novelty is the same for every product and fades. Authenticity depends on the tier, is permanent, and comes with lower price sensitivity (A5).

The experiment behind the ~5% also had plant-based at 23% at equal price. Why doesn't the model? The model is fitted to what plant-based meat actually sells (~1.2%). It predicts about 10% for plant-based meat among mainstream shoppers at equal price and taste. Hypothetical choice experiments tend to overstate adoption of new products. The model uses that experiment only for cultivated meat's starting wariness and price sensitivity.

Why no habit term? Habit cannot be separated from preference without panel data (Heckman [13]). In this model, the slow rollout and fading novelty of Step 4 stand in for habit.

Why is loss aversion symmetric by default? See A3.
