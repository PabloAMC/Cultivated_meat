Words used on this page

A kilo of cells: a kilo of wet cells as harvested from the tank. Mince is taken to be all cells, so it is also a kilo of mince.

Medium: the nutrient liquid the cells grow in (sugar, amino acids, salts, vitamins and a few signalling proteins).

Waste: the ammonia and lactate cells give off as they grow. Both slow growth above a certain level.

Batch and fed-batch: fill the tank, let the cells grow, empty it. In fed-batch, concentrated food is added on the way.

Perfusion: fresh medium flows through the tank all the time and washes the waste out, while a retention device (a filter, a settler or a centrifuge) holds the cells back.

Chemostat: medium flows in and culture flows out all the time, with nothing holding the cells back.

Growth factors: the signalling proteins (such as insulin) that tell cells to grow. They are the dearest part of a medium by weight.

Bubble protectant: a mild detergent (poloxamer) that keeps cells from dying when gas bubbles burst.

Upkeep: the oxygen and sugar a cell uses just to stay alive, whether or not it grows.

mmol a litre, mOsm a kilo, mbar: measures of how much of something is dissolved in the culture. Only their size against the reference values matters here.

kLa: how fast a tank passes oxygen from gas bubbles into the liquid.

Humbird: David Humbird's 2021 study of the process and its costs, the most detailed public analysis of what cultivated meat would cost at scale. Many of the model's starting numbers are his.

Published cells: the best value published for each trait of meat cells, taken from different cell lines.

Long-run share: the share of the meat sold once the product is on every shelf and no longer feels new. It is a ceiling, not a figure for any year.

× the model's estimate: 1.00× leaves the model's own figure; 2.00× doubles it.

Words used on this page

Medium is the nutrient liquid the cells grow in. Waste means the ammonia and lactate that cells give off as they grow. Waste slows growth, so fresh medium must wash it out. Perfusion means that fresh medium flows through the tank all the time. A filter holds the cells back, and they are harvested continuously. A column is a packed tower beside the tank. The culture runs down it in a thin film, and CO₂ leaves without bubbles. Gas the cells tolerate is the speed of gas rising through the tank, in millimetres a second. Bubbles and foam hurt cells, so the cells set how much gas can pass. A run lost is a batch ruined by contamination. It empties the tank and takes days to restart. A draw is one run of the model with its uncertain estimates picked at random from their ranges. The median is the middle draw: half give less and half give more. A most-likely-values figure puts every estimate at its single most likely value instead. A reference point is a fixed set of cells and plant to compare with, not a forecast. Meat buyers who would pick it at the same price is the share of people buying meat who would choose cultivated meat over the conventional kind if the two cost the same and it no longer felt new: 50% means no preference.

What limits each vessel
  • Stirred tank (20 m³ a unit): Oxygen and CO₂ move only through bubbles. Bubbles and foam hurt cells, so the gas flow is capped. A deeper tank passes less gas per litre and clears less CO₂. The stirrer must mix faster than the cells use oxygen.
  • Stirred tank with a bubble cage (20 m³ a unit): The ring's soft bubbling works as in a stirred tank. The cylinder adds gas exchange without bubbles touching the cells, up to what foam allows, but its oxygen and CO₂ reach the cells only with the medium that crosses the mesh, and a mesh fine enough to keep single cells out clogs.
  • Airlift (bubble column) (20 m³ a unit): The same bubble rules apply as in a stirred tank. The tank is taller, so the same gas flow passes less gas per litre and clears less CO₂. The gas mixes the culture, not a stirrer. Its rising gas gives about the power per litre a stirrer gives, so the model holds it to the stirred tank's mixing rule at its own tall shape; no mixing time has been measured in an animal-cell airlift above 2 m³.
  • Single-use bag (5 m³ a unit): The physics is the same as in a stirred tank, at the bag's largest common size. The bag takes no pressure, so the pressure trick does not apply. The frame is cheap, but each run uses a new bag.
  • Packed bed (65 litres a unit): The carriers' surface per litre caps the density. Oxygen falls along the bed, which caps its height. The cells must be detached to harvest, so the bed is grown and emptied in cycles, and some cells stay behind.
  • Hollow fibres (480 mL a unit): The space between the fibres caps the density. Oxygen and food must cross the fibre wall. Cartridges are small, so a plant needs thousands. Cells are flushed out to harvest.

Perfusion assumes a hollow-fibre filter loop that holds the cells back. Its membranes cost about $0.60 per litre they filter. Batch and fed-batch run 2 doublings a cycle from a seed, plus 10 hours to clean and refill (Humbird's design). A lost cycle costs the cycle and 2 more days. The model prices each vessel at the largest unit built for animal cells, up to 20 m³.

What has and has not been measured

Has been measured

  • Dense perfusion at bench scale. CHO cells in a wave bag were held at 100 to 214 million cells a millilitre (Clincke 2013). Chicken cells in a 10-litre tank were held at 80 million a millilitre (SuperMeat 2024, a company report).
  • Ammonia made by cattle meat cells with and without glutamine (Hubalek 2023). With glutamine, the cells made 0.70 mol a kilo and doubled in 26 hours. Without it, they made under 0.14 mol and doubled in 43 to 54 hours.
  • Vow reports growing animal cells in a 20,000-litre food-grade stirred tank, drained and refilled, with no clean room, at about 27 g of cells a litre.
  • A survey of makers found contamination losses of 11.2% of batches, or 19.5% beyond flasks. It did not state run lengths (Powell 2025).
  • Gas flows of 0.1 to 2 mm a second in tanks of 8 to 20 cubic metres with animal cells (Nienow 2006).

Has not been measured

  • Perfusion with animal cells above about 4 cubic metres, and a way to hold cells back at 20.
  • Meat cells denser than about 27 g a litre in a tank of a cubic metre or more.
  • Meat cells that make under 0.14 mol of ammonia a kilo, or under 1.05 mol of lactate per mol of glucose. Bovine cells on a feed without glutamine doubling faster than 42.9 hours.
  • The ammonia and CO₂ that muscle or fish cells tolerate when dense. How much gas and foam dense cultures stand at 20 cubic metres.
  • A pumped column, extra pressure or recycled oxygen with meat cells.
  • The real cost of a food-grade plant for animal cells, with or without shared medium.
  • Medium at $0.20 a litre and growth factors at scale, on a stated mass basis.

Tricks. These are ways to get past the limits below. The model sizes each one by calculation (see the research notes on gas exchange and on waste). Nobody has tried any of them with meat cells.

Feed or process. Cells that make fat from glucose give off CO₂ without using oxygen. This is most of why cells make 1.2 CO₂ per oxygen. Fat in the feed lowers the ratio to 0.97 and raises the CO₂ limit by a quarter (a carbon balance on Humbird's cell). Nobody has tested whether the cells stop making their own fat.

Feed or process. The standard tank's oxygen supply implies about 0.28 mmol/L, which is above air saturation. Pharma holds 30–50% of air saturation, and cells grow unimpaired at about 10–100%. A lower level leaves more room to push oxygen in.

Feed or process. The culture runs as a thin film down half a metre of steel packing, with no bubbles. The model calculates the size, the flow and the cost. Dense cultures have been pumped through fibres at up to about 15–24 tank volumes an hour for weeks, in volumes of 1–4 litres. Nobody has tested more than that. Culture that has lost CO₂ stays alkaline until it is back in the tank, so the more passes an hour, the more of the culture is alkaline at any moment.

A lower value strips more CO₂ per pass but makes the returning culture more alkaline. A value of 55 keeps the pH rise to 0.26.

Feed or process. More pressure dissolves more oxygen. It does nothing for CO₂. Steam-rated tanks take the pressure. Nobody has tested it in cells.

How a shop price becomes a share: the shopper model

The shopper settings, the share at each price, what raises or lowers it, and the same model tried on other products.

For each meal, a shopper picks conventional meat, plant-based meat, cultivated meat, or beans and other whole foods. Each option gets a score that adds up its price, its taste, its health image, whether it is real meat and whether an animal was killed. Tastes vary, so each option's share rises smoothly with its score. Of every hundred shoppers, 95 are mainstream and choose mainly on price, taste and real meat. The other 5 are vegetarian or vegan and care about slaughter. Shoppers also differ in how much they like cultivated meat. Most sit near the typical liking, and a few are far from it in either direction, so some never buy it at any price.

What the model counts. The cost starts from Humbird's 2021 design, which the model reproduces to within about 2%, and adds what he does not count. The share comes from a shopper model fitted to meat and plant-based meat sales.
  • Humbird's design as the start. Counting only what he counts, the model gives $20.65 a kilo of cells for his best estimate, against his own $21.00 (2018 dollars), or $26.99 in 2025 dollars. It reproduces his other four designs as well; the table is under Published cost estimates, and the examples on this page (note).
  • What it adds to his count. Five things, each a lever that starts at zero on his design: thick cultures take up oxygen more slowly, the cells need a least flow of fresh medium, bursting bubbles kill some cells, some cells are lost at harvest, and the medium carries a bubble protectant. Together they bring his design to $30.49 in 2025 dollars.
  • Thick cultures take up oxygen more slowly. A dense culture is more viscous, and the rate at which bubbles pass oxygen into it falls as the viscosity to the power 0.4 (Yagi and Yoshida, 1975). So a tank's oxygen limit falls faster than the culture thickens (note).
  • The plant, tank by tank. The plant's cost is built from what each tank brings with it: the tank, its makeup tanks and filter housings, and its share of the buildings, a rule fitted to Humbird's two plants. A larger tank, or another way of running it, changes the plant with it (note).
  • Maturation. Days in the tank at no growth before harvest, which muscle or fat tissue would need. None by default, because the products on sale or near it are bulk cell mass harvested as soon as it has grown.
  • Cleaning the medium. Ways to take the waste out of used medium so it can be used again, and a leaner solution for the litres that only wash waste out. Both are off until you set them, in section 3 of the first screen (note).
  • Incomes in nominal dollars. Each region's income is its GDP per head in current US dollars, the same units as its meat prices (A2).
  • Plant-based meat by volume. The shopper model is fitted to plant-based meat's share of meat by volume, 0.4%: 0.7% of US meat sales by value, at a price 77% above the conventional meat it copies [14].
  • Conventional meat's price responds. As cultivated meat takes a share of a region's meat, conventional meat's prices fall, by the factor (1 − share) to the power 1/(supply elasticity − demand elasticity), with a supply elasticity of 2 and a demand elasticity of -0.65. The cheaper meat holds the share back, most where the share is large.

Step 1 · From cost to price. The cost of a kilo of cells is medium, plant and losses. The shop price adds the costs of reaching the shelf and the shop's margin. With every estimate at its most likely value that is 5.9 times the price of US minced beef or pork cuts ($12 a kilo) for the low-lactate example and 1.1 times for the all-favourable example.

The price ratio R is cultivated meat's price in the shop divided by the price of the conventional meat beside it. A ratio of 1 means the same price, and 2 means twice the price. The shop price has three parts:

  • Medium is the nutrient liquid the cells grow in. Cells must eat a fixed amount of amino acids, glucose and salts, and that feed costs at least about $2.0 a kilo of cells [2]. The salts, buffer and vitamins in that figure are an assumption. How many litres carry the feed depends on the waste the cells make. Fresh medium must wash ammonia out before it passes 4 mmol a litre, the level the reference points' cells are taken to tolerate, and lactate likewise. The model works out the glucose a kilo of cells takes up from the cells' own balance: what they build, what they burn and what they turn into lactate. Grown in batches, the published cells stop thin, held by their lactate, so each kilo leaves with about 108 litres of medium. The low-lactate example needs 39, set by ammonia, and the all-favourable example 12. Companies reported medium at $0.20 a litre or less in 2025, and an amino-acid cost analysis by GFI and MG Consulting supports that level [3]. Nobody has verified it at production scale.
  • The plant covers capital, labour, energy and supplies. Its cost per kilo is its cost per cubic metre of tank divided by what a cubic metre makes in a year. The second part is physics: how much oxygen a cell needs, and how much oxygen and CO₂ a tank can exchange without bubbles killing the cells. The plant's cost per cubic metre comes from Humbird's design [2] in 2025 prices, item by item. His plant as drawn comes to $14.5/kg in 2018 prices. In perfusion a filter holds the cells back. Its membranes are paid for by the litre filtered, and they are the largest single line for the low-lactate example. Scaling up animal cells is hard because of oxygen and CO₂ transfer and sterility [2].
  • Getting it to the shelf adds $2.5/kg for forming, packing and cold chain, and then the shop's margin, 28% of the shelf price. Fitted to conventional meat's wholesale-to-retail spreads, this form gives the shop more dollars on a dearer product, as shops' percentage margins do. A structured cut is 80% cells bound with plant protein, plus $2/kg to assemble it. No study has costed this for cultivated meat. The figures come from plant-protein extrusion, surimi and binder prices [6].

Step 2 · From price to choice. Shoppers choose among four options. They weigh price, taste, health, “is it real meat?” and “no slaughter”. The share of meals in which shoppers choose cultivated meat at equal price, once it is familiar, is a setting: 13% for Published cells and the low-lactate example, 55% for the all-favourable example. Counted among the meals that contain meat only, these are 14.4% and 58.7%: the figure the first screen calls meat buyers who would pick it at the same price.

This is a standard discrete-choice model [12]. For each meal, a shopper picks conventional meat, plant-based meat, cultivated meat, or beans and other whole foods. The shares therefore include the bean meals. Each option gets a score that adds up its price, taste, health, whether it is real meat and whether an animal was killed. Tastes vary, so the top score does not always win. Each option's share rises smoothly with its score, and one more point means about 2.7 times as often.

Two kinds of shopper. 95% are mainstream, and they choose mainly on price, taste and real meat. 5% are vegetarian or vegan [7]. They care about slaughter and mostly eat beans. 7% of shoppers, counted among the mainstream, are the committed buyers of plant-based meat: they buy 82% of the mainstream's plant-based meat and respond a quarter as strongly to its price. Within each kind, shoppers differ in how much they like cultivated meat: most sit near the typical liking, and a few are far from it in either direction (A1).

Tied to data. Price sensitivity follows grocery data for meat (1% dearer, 0.9% fewer purchases [4]). It is steeper for cultivated meat because an almost identical product is next to it (A1). Three weights are solved so that the model reproduces plant-based meat's share (0.4% of meat by volume) and ~89% mainstream buyers [14], plus an assumed ~6% of mainstream meals that skip meat (A4). Taste sets the scale for the rest. As a check, the same model with only the product's facts changed to plant-based milk's predicts about 15%, close to milk's share (see the chart of other products in How a shop price becomes a share). This is a weak test, because the milk facts are set by hand. A harder test. Six premium animal products (organic milk, cage-free, organic and omega-3 eggs, organic chicken and organic meat) take 2–7% of sales at 1.5 to 2.2 times the conventional price. Each is the ordinary product plus a virtue, which is what cultivated meat above equal price would be. With cultivated meat 8.5 times as price-sensitive as meat in general (the model's setting, written κ in A1), the model reproduces them to within a factor of about 2.6. It over-predicted them sixfold at its earlier setting of 4. For example, it gives about 21% for a real-meat product at 1.44× the price of chicken cuts, and about 1% at 2.6× mince. It still over-predicts at small premiums and under-predicts at large ones. Real premium buyers look like a small group that pays whatever the premium, and one price-sensitivity number cannot capture that.

At first contact the model gives 4% at equal price (Step 4). Price is the main limit on the share. At the shop price of the low-lactate example, 5.9 times US minced beef or pork cuts ($12 a kilo), a US shopper chooses cultivated mince 0.5% of the time in the long run.

Step 3 · Every meat, every region. The model repeats the choice at each meat's local price and adds up the results. For the low-lactate example the median of the draws is 0.2% of the world's meat and seafood by weight, 0.3% in the United States and 0.5% in Europe.

Cultivated meat costs about the same whatever animal it copies, but conventional prices vary. In the US, chicken mince costs about $5/kg, a beef steak $20 and sushi-grade fish $40. So the model runs each meat at its local price and adds up the results by weight (closest to the climate footprint) and by value (the market). Neither total counts animals. Most land animals raised for meat are chickens, and cultivated meat does worst there.

Mince, cuts and premium products. Shoppers like a cultivated cut less than cultivated mince, for two reasons. First, a cut is cells bound with plant protein, so it gets that much less of the credit shoppers give real meat: at 80% cells the penalty is −1.06 points of score, and it grows as the share of cells falls. The same share of cells sets the cut's cost. Second, wanting the genuine article: nobody misses “the real thing” in a nugget, but for wagyu the breed is the product. A further penalty starts at 2 times the species' cheapest form and reaches its full size, −1.1 points, at 4 times (wagyu, sushi-grade fish). Its size is a judgement, scaled by one setting that the draws take from 0.5 to 1.5. Every product has the same price sensitivity. The charts label a cut “premium” from 2.5 times its species' cheapest form. The label changes no number.

What comes out (the chart of share by type of meat in Share by type of meat, by region and over time):

  • Beef and seafood are where cultivated meat can compete. Chicken and pork stay dearer even in the all-favourable example.
  • Premium products get the biggest share of their own category, because cultivated meat is nearest their price. Wanting the genuine article limits that share, and the markets are small.
  • Beef cuts and fish fillets displace the most meat, in every case and region.

Regions. Europe is easiest, because meat is dear and shoppers are rich: the low-lactate example's median there is 0.5%, against 0.3% in the United States and 0.2% in China. Cheap chicken and pork lower the figures for China and for the world. Low-income regions are hardest, because meat is cheap and shoppers are price-sensitive (A2). The prices and mixes for low-income regions are rough (A7).

Markets closed by law, and conventional meat's price. Every share is of the region's whole market, and the parts closed by law buy none (see “What the model counts”). As cultivated meat takes sales, conventional meat gets cheaper, which holds the share back. At the low-lactate example's share the effect is too small to see; it matters only when the share is large.

Step 4 · Over time. The share starts near zero. It rises as the product spreads and stops feeling new. One product sold at the price of the conventional meat it copies gets nine tenths of the way in about 24 years.
  • Rollout. Availability spreads like it does for other new products, first among early adopters and then by word of mouth (Bass diffusion [11], with standard rates from durable goods).
  • Familiarity. In a US choice experiment, only ~5% chose lab-grown meat at the same price as beef [8]. The model starts about there (4% at equal price) and lets the wariness fade with exposure.
  • Repeat buying. 62% of first purchases turn into repeat buying, 14% of buyers stop each year and those who stopped try again as others first try. The figures are plant-based meat's (GFI, SPINS consumer panel, 2021-25). They slow the climb but leave the long-run share as it is.

Worked example (a US shopper, and one cultivated product always sold at the price of the conventional meat it copies). The share starts from nothing at launch. As a share of the meat sold it is 4% after 10 years and 14% after 30. The long-run ceiling is 14% of the meat sold (13% of all choices). The share reaches nine tenths of it in year 24. The chart in Share by type of meat, by region and over time draws the same curve for your settings' whole market, and prints its own year.

For timing, the biggest unknown is how wary people are at the start. Surveys range from ~5% (a cold choice experiment) to ~60% (“cultivated chicken in a restaurant”) [10]. The “starting wariness” setting runs from 3% at −3.5 to 23% at +1.5, with 13% at 0 (each at equal price). The warmest survey figure, ~60%, would need about +3.0, beyond the setting's range. It changes how fast the share grows, not how far. Plant-based meat follows the same rules but stalls, because its taste and price gaps do not fade.

Which products first. Cultivated meat already costs less than some luxury products, but those markets are small. It costs more than commodity meat, even in the all-favourable example.

A luxury price is mostly not cost. A5 wagyu or caviar is dear because of breed, origin and scarcity (economic rent). Cultivated meat can copy the meat but not the pedigree. So the model compares its cost with the product's ordinary grade (farmed salmon, crossbred wagyu). A prestige core of buyers never switches. The setting is 25%, a proxy taken from the only two published splits (wild salmon and bellota ibérico).

Cultivated meat is already cheaper than foie gras, bluefin tuna, sea urchin and wagyu, and lobster is close. Each of these markets is only thousands to hundreds of thousands of tonnes a year. Commodity beef, pork and chicken are tens of millions of tonnes each, and the chart of which products it could enter first uses whole-category volumes. At world prices cultivated meat costs more than these. New technologies often start at the top and move down-market as costs fall [18], [19]. Foie gras stands out. It is unstructured, it is expensive, and more places are banning it on welfare grounds that do not apply to a cultivated version.

That chart gives luxury products higher shares than the chart of share by type of meat does. It removes the prestige core and treats the rest like ordinary cuts. The chart of share by type of meat applies one penalty to the whole category, and that is the conservative view. The entry chart assumes sale at cost, so it shows reach and impact, not profit.

What would change the conclusions. Cheaper classes of plant, better cells or cheap medium at scale would raise the share. Low density at scale or more lost runs would lower it. How shoppers react could change it either way.

  • Down: cells that reach only half the density in a pharma-style plant (the table in Step 1 gives that cost), or more runs lost to contamination than the reference points assume. The “Runs lost” setting covers a chance of 0.2% to 2% a day.

A0. The equations

Step 1, the price ratio.

\[ R \;=\; \frac{\Big(\overbrace{\iota\,\eta\,p_{\rm med}}^{\text{medium}} \;+\; \overbrace{h}^{\text{plant}} \;+\; \overbrace{k}^{\text{assembly (cuts)}} \;+\; \overbrace{a}^{\text{to the shelf}}\Big)\Big/\big(1-\overbrace{m}^{\text{shop's margin}}\big)}{p_c\,t} \tag{1} \]

This is the form for the process a company reports. The cases compute the cost of the cells from the tank physics instead (Step 1), and the rest of the equation is the same. \(\iota\): litres of medium per kilo; \(\eta\): cell efficiency (1 = as reported); \(p_{\rm med}\): medium price; \(h\): plant running cost; \(k\): assembling a cut (cuts only). For a cut the cell cost (medium plus plant) is multiplied by \(c\), the share of the cut that is cells, and the plant protein that makes up the rest is added at its price; \(a\): forming, packing and cold chain per kilo; \(m\): the shop's gross margin as a share of the shelf price; \(p_c\): conventional price; \(t\): meat-tax multiplier. The medium cost never falls below the feedstock dissolved in it.

Step 2, the score and the shares.

\[ V_j \;=\; \underbrace{\alpha\ln(y_{\rm eff}-p_j) \;-\; \lambda\,(d_j)^{+} + (d_j)^{-}}_{\text{price}} \;+\; \underbrace{w^{t}(a_j-1)}_{\text{taste}} \;+\; \underbrace{w^{rt}\,b_j}_{\text{real meat}} \;+\; \underbrace{w^{s}\,g_j}_{\text{no slaughter}} \;+\; \underbrace{w^{h}\,\zeta_j}_{\text{health}} \;+\; \underbrace{\nu_j+\tau_j}_{\text{novelty; cut, premium}} \tag{2} \] \[ P_j = \frac{e^{V_j}}{\sum_k e^{V_k}}, \qquad \text{share}_j = w_{\rm eth}\,P^{\rm ethical}_j + (1-w_{\rm eth})\,P^{\rm mainstream}_j \tag{3} \]

Price enters twice. First, through the dollars it takes from income: \(p_j = R_j\,p_c\), and \(y_{\rm eff}\) is an income measure (A2). Second, through the premium over conventional meat, \(d_j = R_j-1\). The premium is split into its positive part \((d_j)^{+}\) and its negative part \((d_j)^{-}\). At the default \(\lambda=1\) the two terms add up to \(-d_j\) (A3). \(a_j\): taste (1 = as good as conventional); \(b_j\), \(g_j\): 1 if real meat, 1 if slaughter-free; \(\zeta_j\): health image. The weights \(w\) (A4) differ between the two kinds of shopper for no slaughter, real meat and health. \(\nu_j\) (novelty) is zero unless set. \(\tau_j\) is how much less shoppers like a cultivated cut or premium product than cultivated mince (A5). For cultivated meat the score also carries each shopper's own liking, which is spread around a mean set so that the share at equal price comes out at its setting (A1).

Step 3, adding up.

\[ \text{share}_{\rm vol} = \sum_i \frac{\omega_i}{\sum_k \omega_k}\,s_i, \qquad \text{share}_{\rm val} = \sum_i \frac{p_i\,\omega_i}{\sum_k p_k\,\omega_k}\,s_i \tag{4} \]

\(s_i\): cultivated's share of meat type \(i\); \(\omega_i\): that type's share of meat eaten (the listed weights sum to slightly more than 1 in some regions, hence the normalisation); \(p_i\): its local price.

Step 4, over time.

\[ \text{share}(t) = F(t)\times \text{ceiling}\big(\nu(t)\big), \qquad \nu(t) = \nu_x + (\nu_{x0}-\nu_x)\,e^{-r\,E(t)} \tag{5} \]

\(F(t)\): the fraction of the market reached, a Bass curve with rates \(p_{\rm B}\) (early adopters) and \(q_{\rm B}\) (word of mouth). Novelty \(\nu\) fades from its starting value \(\nu_{x0}\) toward its long-run value \(\nu_x\) as cumulative exposure \(E(t)\) grows, at speed \(r\). The ceiling is the Step 2 share at that novelty.

A1. Price sensitivity: where the price weight comes from

Grocery data give the price elasticity of meat as a category, \(\varepsilon \approx -0.9\) [4]. This means that meat 1% dearer sells 0.9% less. Meat as a whole has no close substitute, so it is fairly insensitive to price. A single cultivated product does have a close substitute, which is conventional meat on the same shelf. So a single product is more sensitive than the category. At the calibration price the sensitivity is \(4 \times (-0.9) = -3.6\), the usual gap between a product and its category. That sets the price weight that every product shares. Cultivated meat's price difference from the conventional meat beside it then counts \(\kappa/4\) times as much again, because the two are near-identical (\(\kappa = 8.5\) by default). The model solves the price weight \(\beta\) (the slope of the price score) so that it delivers exactly that sensitivity at the calibration price \(p_x\) and at cultivated meat's share there, \(s_x\). These two depend on each other, so the model solves them together:

\[ \beta=\frac{\kappa\,\varepsilon}{p_x\,(1-s_x)}+\frac{\lambda}{p_c},\qquad \alpha=-\beta\,(y_{\rm ref}-p_x) \tag{A1} \]

The \(\lambda/p_c\) term adds back the part of the price response that the reference-price term in Eq. (2) already contains. So \(\kappa\) sets the level of sensitivity, and \(\lambda\) sets only its shape around parity. The price \(p_x\) is a fixed calibration price of $29/kg. The model holds it fixed, so that correcting a cost changes cultivated meat's price but not how shoppers respond to price. At a higher shop price the model is more price-sensitive.

Evidence for \(\kappa\) and for the spread of liking. There are two kinds. First, Van Loo, Caputo and Lusk [8] priced lab-grown meat at six levels. The paper reports no elasticity; simulating its fitted model gives about −1.5 at equal price (−1.1 to −1.65 across its four groups). Its main finding is that shoppers differ widely in how much they like lab-grown meat: about half prefer it to buying nothing, half do not. With every shopper alike this model gave −3.3 in that setting. With the spread of liking added in November 2026 it gives -1.5, and matches the experiment at all four prices tested. The spread is a Student's t with a standard deviation of 3.55 points of score and 7 degrees of freedom: most shoppers sit near the typical liking, and a few are far from it in either direction. It is fitted to this experiment and to the shares that premium real meats hold at two to four times the ordinary price, together. Second, premium animal products show what share a product takes when it is the conventional one plus a virtue, at a higher price. Organic milk, cage-free, organic and omega-3 eggs, organic chicken and organic meat take 2–7% of volume at 1.5 to 2.2 times the conventional price. At \(\kappa = 4\), this model's default until November 2026, the model over-predicts those six about sixfold. At 8.5 the typical miss is under threefold, and the best fit is at 10. So 8.5 is the best fit to the premium products within what the experiment allows. \(\kappa\) changes nothing at equal price. Once shoppers are familiar with the product, the model's elasticity is about −1.3 at parity, −2.0 at \(R=1.5\) and −2.5 at \(R=2.4\). At one and a half times the price of the conventional meat it copies, \(\kappa=4\) gives a share of about 9%, 8.5 about 7% and 10 about 6%. \(\kappa\) plays the role that a nested logit's similarity parameter would play for a “real meat” nest, but it applies to price only.

A2. Income

The income part of the price score, \(\alpha\ln(y_{\rm eff}-p_j)\), has the Berry–Levinsohn–Pakes form [12]. The same premium is a bigger bite of a smaller income, so poorer shoppers are more sensitive to price. Income is GDP per head in nominal US dollars (World Bank, 2024), because the meat prices it is set against are nominal dollars too. Taken literally, the form makes poor shoppers far more price-sensitive than food data show. So the model damps income, \(y_{\rm eff}=y_{\rm ref}\,(y/y_{\rm ref})^{\phi}\), with \(\phi=0.32\), chosen so that a shopper in Nigeria is about 2.2 times as price-sensitive as one in the US at equal price, the gap in the data [15]. (\(\phi=0\) removes income. US results do not depend on \(\phi\).) For the same product at one and a half times the price of the conventional meat it copies, this gives 7% in the US, 5% in China and 3% in Nigeria. Wealth relaxes price sensitivity but does not erase the other differences. Even a very rich shopper buys cultivated meat only 8% of the time at that price.

A3. Loss aversion (symmetric by default)

People judge a price against the familiar price of the conventional product, and a premium can feel like a loss [13]. The term \(-\lambda(d_j)^{+}+(d_j)^{-}\) rewards a discount at rate 1 and penalises a premium at rate \(\lambda\). The default \(\lambda=1\) is symmetric, and the slider goes up to Tversky and Kahneman's 2.25. The model leaves it symmetric by default for three reasons. First, it barely changes the result once \(\beta\) is re-fitted (at one and a half times the price of the conventional meat it copies, 7% at \(\lambda=1\) against 5% at 2.25). Second, cultivated meat data cannot identify it. Third, measured loss aversion largely reflects differences in price sensitivity between shoppers (Bell and Lattin, 2000), and \(\kappa\) already contains those.

A4. Calibration, and the attribute weights

Fixed from data: plant-based meat's price premium (+77% [14]) and taste (0.8 [7]), and the ethical share (5% [7]). Solved: the mainstream real-meat weight and the two health weights, so that the model reproduces (i) the ~89% of plant-based buyers who are mainstream [14], (ii) ~6% of mainstream meals skipping meat by choice, and (iii) the ethical shoppers' small plant-based share. Together these give plant-based meat its observed share, 0.4% of meat by volume. Moving the ethical share, \(\lambda\), \(\kappa\) or income re-solves them live, so plant-based stays anchored.

Only differences between scores matter in this kind of model, so one weight has to set the scale. Taste sets it (\(w^t = 5\)). This is anchored to willingness-to-pay studies that rank taste first (taste about twice health and three times safety, Malone and Lusk 2017). Each weight slider shows its size relative to taste. The solved mainstream health weight comes out at 0.46× taste, which is lighter than the ~0.5× in those studies. Beans' appeal comes from their health score (+2) times a solved weight, not from a free constant. The data pin down only the product of the two. Without this, plant-based meat would roughly triple, with mostly vegetarian buyers, which contradicts the data.

A5. Three different “is it real?” questions, and how much less shoppers like cuts and premium products
  • Real meat (\(b\)) asks whether it is animal tissue. The answer is permanent and the same for every product, and it is the reason cultivated meat takes buyers from conventional meat.
  • Novelty (\(\nu\)) asks whether it is new, strange or safe. It is the same for a nugget and a steak, and it fades with familiarity (Step 4).
  • The genuine article (\(\tau\)) asks whether, for this occasion, the shopper wants the original. It depends on the product and is permanent. It is irrelevant for a nugget and decisive for wagyu.

They are separate because they behave differently. A cultivated nugget faces novelty but nobody wants the original nugget. A cultivated wagyu faces both. A fourth attribute, health image (\(\zeta\)), is separate again. It is 0 by default, because surveys find both a “clean meat” appeal and an “ultra-processed” aversion. The draws take it between −0.5 and +0.5.

In formulas. For mince and other unstructured products \(\tau=0\). For a cut made of a share \(c\) of cells, priced at \(q\) times its species' cheapest form:

\[ \tau=\underbrace{-(1-c)\,w^{rt}_M}_{\text{part plant protein}}\;+\;\rho\,\tau_{\rm prem}\, \min\!\Big(1,\max\!\Big(0,\frac{\ln(q/2)}{\ln(4/2)}\Big)\Big) \tag{A5} \]

The first term is derived: \(w^{rt}_M\) is the weight mainstream shoppers put on real meat (A4), and a cut that is one-fifth plant protein gets four-fifths of that credit. \(c\) is the same share of cells that sets the cut's cost in Eq. (1). The second term is a judgement: \(\tau_{\rm prem}=−1.1\) is the full penalty on a cultivated version of a premium product, and \(\rho\) scales it. It rises smoothly with price, so no product changes sharply at a threshold, and because \(q\) is taken per species every species can have premium products. Wagyu and sushi-grade fish are well attested. Organic chicken and heritage pork are real but smaller, so they have little volume. Price sensitivity is the same for every product. Until 19 November 2026 the model also made buyers of cuts and premium products less sensitive to price [5]; that raised cultivated meat's share wherever it was the dearer product, and it was removed.

A6. Entry points: the equations behind the chart of which products it could enter first

A product's headline price splits into the accessible price and the rent. The model takes cultivated meat's price ratio against the accessible price only (Rosen's hedonic pricing [16]):

\[ p^{\rm auth} = p^{\rm base} + \underbrace{(p^{\rm auth}-p^{\rm base})}_{\text{rent}},\qquad R = \frac{c_x}{p^{\rm base}},\qquad c_x = c_{\rm bio} + k\,[\text{cut}] + m \tag{A6a} \]

A prestige core, a share \(\chi\) of the category's volume \(Q\), pays the rent and never switches. Luxury sellers keep their price and give up volume rather than discount, which is the classic Veblen pattern [17]. Only the rest is reachable. Cultivated meat wins a share \(s\) of it from the same shopper model as Step 2, using \(\tau\) for mince or for a cut (A5) without the premium part. (The premium part stands for the prestige core, which the model has already removed.)

\[ Q^{\rm reach} = (1-\chi)\,Q,\qquad s = S\big(R;\ \tau_{\rm mince\ or\ cut}\big),\qquad D = s\cdot Q^{\rm reach}\quad(\text{kt/yr displaced}) \tag{A6b} \]

Zones. A product is about equal if the accessible price is within $3 of cultivated meat's cost. It is cheaper now if the accessible price is above that. It is only in the all-favourable example if the accessible price is above the cost of the all-favourable example. Otherwise it is never on price. A single-grade product (no rent, \(\chi=0\)) reproduces the chart of share by type of meat exactly, so the two views agree. The model counts wanting the genuine article once, as removed volume for the prestige grade and through \(\tau\) for the accessible grade. The model has limits. It does not model margin or profit, its shopper model is calibrated on meat (so it is indicative for luxury and seafood), and it treats each product in isolation. The accessible and headline prices come from sources for each product (June 2026 retail and wholesale, with the basis shown when you hover over a bubble). Shark fin's price is an estimate.

The markup is part per kilo and part a percentage. Forming, packing, cold chain and transport cost the same per kilo whatever the product's price. USDA's monthly meat prices for 1970–2025 show this. Over one to five years, shop prices move about dollar for dollar with wholesale prices. A pure percentage markup would move them 1.7–2.5 times as much. The shop's own margin is a percentage: US meat departments run at about 28–32% of the shelf price, so a product that costs several times more carries more dollars of margin. Fitted to USDA's 2025 spreads with a 28% margin, the per-kilo part is $1.6 for chicken and $2.9 for beef. Until November 2026 the model added a flat $5. At the low-lactate example's most-likely-values cost, the shelf price is $71 under this rule, $54 under the old flat $5, and $90 if the whole spread were a percentage. Both parts have sliders.

A8. Questions a sceptic might ask

Isn't 13% at equal price just assumed? Yes. It is a judgement, and the model says so. Until November 2026 two real meats at the same price split the market about evenly, which no survey supports. The share is now a setting with a wide range (3% to 53% in the draws), the first screen's chart shows the answer across that range, and Which settings change the cost and the share most ranks it against the cost.

Why not a nested logit? The shared real-meat attribute and the two kinds of shopper already make cultivated take its share almost entirely from conventional meat, which is what a nest would do, with fewer unobservable parameters.

Is there really no fitted constant? Almost none. Beans' appeal is a labelled attribute (health) times a solved weight, not a free constant. The model assumes the attribute's position (+2), so only the product of the two is identified (A4).

Isn't the premium penalty just novelty again? No. Novelty is the same for every product and fades. Wanting the genuine article depends on how premium the product is, and is permanent (A5).

The experiment behind the ~5% also had plant-based at 23% at equal price. Why doesn't the model? The model is fitted to what plant-based meat actually sells (0.4% of meat by volume). It predicts about 0.6% for plant-based meat among mainstream shoppers at equal price and taste. Hypothetical choice experiments tend to overstate adoption of new products. The model uses that experiment only for cultivated meat's starting wariness and price sensitivity.

Why no habit term? Habit cannot be separated from preference without panel data (Heckman [13]). In this model, the slow rollout and fading novelty of Step 4 stand in for habit.

Why is loss aversion symmetric by default? See A3.
