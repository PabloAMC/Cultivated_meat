# Steel is proven; continuous harvest isn't

The 20 m3 sterile tank your floor relies on is real hardware, but running it continuously is not yet a demonstrated practice for animal cells, and the floor's productivity assumption is the weakest link, not the vessel. Fill-and-draw is the only one of the three modes with any 20 m3 precedent (Vow filed it as an operating option, without run-length or density data); a retention-free chemostat has never been a commercial animal-cell process at any scale; and perfusion with cell retention tops out at 2-5 m3 in pharma and at 10 L in the published cultivated-meat record. The research notes put about **65%** on a 20 m3 retention-free continuous or fill-and-draw process being technically operable for 2-4 week runs, about **25%** on it sustaining more than 50 g/L wet without major metabolic engineering, and about **10%** on pharma-style perfusion being practical at 20 m3 with current hardware. Your "continuous" tier multiplies the fed-batch end-of-run density (120 g/L) by the maximum growth rate (24 h doubling) at 91% uptime, which gives about 22 t per m3 per year; that is an upper bound on the mode, roughly three to six times what Vow has even targeted from its 20 m3-class line. The $14/kg sterile floor is therefore better read as "$14 if everything about the biology works at once", with a realistic range of roughly $16-30/kg for the same plant (central $20-25), and the $5/kg floor requires a plant class (non-axenic, fermentation-style) for which no animal-cell evidence exists (about 10% by 2035). Between 2023 and 2026 the sector produced one genuine scale milestone (Vow, 20,000 L, batch or draw-and-fill), one peer-reviewed bench perfusion paper, one company white paper at 10 L, and a string of cancelled or failed large plants. The model should gain three things: an explicit productivity derate for retention-free modes, a run-length and failure-hazard term replacing the fixed 91% uptime, and an intermediate "de-pharma'd but axenic" plant class at about $200-400k per m3.

## The 20 m3 tank exists; nobody has run one continuously with animal cells

Three modes sit behind your "continuous" label, and they have very different pedigrees. In **fill-and-draw** (repeated batch) you drain most of the tank, leave a fraction as inoculum, and refill. In a **chemostat** you feed and bleed continuously with no device holding cells back, so cells leave at the rate they grow. In **perfusion** a filter or settler retains the cells while spent medium is washed out, which permits much higher density but adds hardware that must scale with flow.

Sterile stirred tanks for suspension animal cells are demonstrated to **20-25 m3** in fed-batch antibody manufacture ([BioPharm International](https://biopharminternational.com/view/25-year-retrospective-mammalian-cell-culture); [Pharma's Almanac](https://www.pharmasalmanac.com/nice-insight/articles/is-the-bioreactor-arms-race-over)). A retention-free mode needs no hardware beyond that tank, so in vessel-engineering terms it scales like fed-batch; what is undemonstrated is weeks of continuous sterile operation. A **50 m3** animal-cell vessel has not been run; Humbird treats it as a modelled optimum, and he also finds that above 20 m3 CO2 removal erodes the achievable density ([Humbird 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8362201)).

Perfusion is where the scale ceiling is hard. The largest documented commercial perfusion bioreactors are **4,000 L** units at Genzyme's Geel plant ([Pharmaceutical Technology](https://www.pharmaceutical-technology.com/projects/genzyme/)); trade and peer-reviewed sources treat about 2,000 L as manufacturing scale, already needing several hollow-fibre devices in parallel ([Romann et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.1002/bit.28589)); and the largest vendor rating is 5,000 L, a supplier claim without independent data ([Repligen](https://www.repligen.com/technologies/xcell-atf/XCell-ATF-Devices-and-Controllers)). Humbird's sizing, one ATF-10 unit per roughly 1,000 L per day, is why his perfusion plant is 96 tanks of 2 m3 ([Humbird 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8362201)). By the same arithmetic a 20 m3 tank at one vessel volume per day needs about 20 such units and a 50 m3 tank about 50, which nobody has reported; Pasitka's own supplementary tables price 50 ATF-10 units at $17.5 million for their theoretical 50,000 L facility and lean instead on two unvalidated "large-scale units" ([Nature Food](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43016-024-01022-w)). Your decision to keep retention filters out of the 20 m3 tiers is well founded. The consequence is that those tiers cannot borrow perfusion densities.

The chemostat is the mode with the thinnest record. Mammalian chemostats are documented only at lab scale, at around 10^6 cells per mL and dilution rates of 0.25-0.35 per day ([J Gen Virol 1973](https://www.microbiologyresearch.org/content/journal/jgv/10.1099/0022-1317-20-1-29); [PMC1058198](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1058198)), and every documented commercial "continuous" animal-cell process uses retention ([Bayer/ECI](https://dc.engconfintl.org/biomanufact_ii/31); [Remicade label](https://www.Drugs.Com/pro/remicade.html)). The notes put about 80% on no commercial retention-free animal-cell chemostat ever having existed in pharma, with one unchecked exception class: veterinary-vaccine BHK processes run as repeated batch in multi-m3 tanks, which could not be sourced and are worth a manual literature check. Pharma abandoned continuous culture for product-economics reasons (rising fed-batch titres, batch definition), not because of a proven physical barrier ([BioPharm International 2016](https://www.biopharminternational.com/view/perfusion-21st-century)), and for a biomass product the classic objection, that fast-growing non-producers take over, becomes harmless. That argues the mode is possible, not that it is shown.

| Mode | Largest scale shown with animal cells | Main unknown at 20-50 m3 | Credence it works at 20 m3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fill-and-draw | 20,000 L filed as an option by Vow (no cycle count or density published); routine in seed trains | Cycles before forced restart; density per cycle | About 75-80% for multi-cycle runs (my split of the notes' 65%) |
| Chemostat, no retention | Lab scale, about 10^6 cells/mL | Steady-state density; sterility over weeks; washout margin | About 45-50% (my split) |
| Perfusion with retention | 4,000 L pharma; 10 L cultivated meat | Retention hardware does not exist at this flow | About 10% with current hardware (notes) |

The notes' combined figure for the first two rows is 65%; the split is my judgement, on the reasoning that each fill-and-draw cycle is a fed-batch-like event already proven at this size, whereas a chemostat adds a continuous sterile feed and an untested steady state.

## The floor multiplies peak density by peak growth rate, which no retention-free mode delivers

In `floor_physics.py` the continuous tiers produce `0.8 x 120 g/L x ln2/24 h x 8760 h x 0.91`, about **22 t of wet cells per m3 of vessel per year**, equivalent to 83 g per litre of working volume per day. Three separate optimisms are stacked in that product.

First, density and growth rate are taken at their maxima simultaneously. In fill-and-draw the culture spends each cycle climbing back to the cap, so average output is the fraction drawn divided by the regrowth time. For a draw fraction f this is f / ln(1/(1-f)) of the model's figure: **72% at a 50% draw and 50% at the 80% draw** that patents describe as typical ([US 9,212,214](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9212214)) (my arithmetic). A chemostat must run below the maximum growth rate to avoid washout; with a maximum of about 0.7 per day the notes estimate a practical dilution rate of 0.3-0.6 per day, or 43-87% of the model's figure, before any growth slowdown from accumulated waste.

Second, 120 g/L is Humbird's ceiling for a hypothetical metabolically enhanced cell line. For wild-type metabolism his fed-batch limit is about **7 g/L**, set by ammonia ([Humbird 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8362201)). A retention-free culture removes waste only with the cells, so it is waste-limited exactly as a batch is, and at half a vessel volume of exchange per day it is worse off than Humbird's perfusion cases. What has been reported without retention is lower and softer: Vow's record 538 kg harvest from a 20,000 L vessel implies about 27 g/L if the vessel was full ([FoodNavigator](https://www.foodnavigator-usa.com/Article/2025/04/10/cultivated-meat-and-fat-inches-toward-retail/)); investor and press sources claim 60-90 g/L and 55-100 g/L from unpublished company data ([AgFunderNews](https://agfundernews.com/humbird-was-spectacularly-wrong-on-cultivated-meat-economics-says-report-as-vow-predicts-it-will-soon-be-unit-margin-positive); [Protein Production Technology](https://proteinproductiontechnology.com/post/parima-and-vows-22-000l-breakthrough-signals-cultivated-meats-next-commercial-chapter)). None of these states water content, and GFI's 2025 review warns that overestimated mass per cell has inflated g/L figures across earlier analyses ([GFI](https://gfi.org/cm-tea-report)). The 50 m3 tier carries the same 120 g/L, although Humbird's modelled maximum falls from about 150 g/L at 20 m3 to about 70 g/L at 200 m3 ([Humbird report](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/download/1438/2973/2173)); a crude log-linear interpolation takes a fifth off at 50 m3.

Third, the 24 h doubling time is Humbird's assumption, not a measurement on a food cell line in a bioreactor. Published figures cluster at **33-61 h**: SuperMeat reports 0.5 doublings per day during bleed ([SuperMeat](https://supermeat.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/A-Deep-Dive-Into-Cost-Effective-Cultivated-Meat-Production-V.pdf)), bovine satellite cells about 50 h ([Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-97813-7)), and your own Pasitka-derived input is 61 h.

The best available reality check is Vow's stated target of 130,000 kg per year from a facility with a 20,000 L main vessel and 35,000 L in total ([FoodNavigator](https://www.foodnavigator-usa.com/Article/2025/04/10/cultivated-meat-and-fat-inches-toward-retail/); [GFI 2026](https://gfi.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/GFI-2026-State-of-the-Industry-report-Cultivated-meat-seafood-and-ingredients.pdf)). That is **3.7-6.5 t per m3 per year, 17-29% of the model's 22**, and it is a company target, not an achieved rate. So yes, the density-times-rate assumption for retention-free culture is too generous as a central case; it is defensible only as a bound, which is how the tier should be labelled.

## What 2023-2026 actually delivered: one real 20 m3 line and a trail of unbuilt giants

The public record separates cleanly into three evidence grades.

| Grade | What it covers | Scale |
|---|---|---|
| Demonstrated (regulatory filing, independent tracker, peer review) | Vow production at 20,000 L, batch or draw-and-fill; Pasitka TFF perfusion at 130 x 10^6 cells/mL with harvests over 20 days; "dozens of runs" at 2,000 L at UPSIDE; 2,000 L trial runs at Joes Future Food | 20 m3 batch; bench perfusion |
| Self-reported, unreviewed | SuperMeat 10 L perfusion with 45-day bleed; PARIMA tonne-scale duck run on Vow's 22,000 L line; Ark Biotech 1,250 L airlift run | 10 L to 22 m3 |
| Modelled or announced only | Pasitka 50,000 L facility at $6.2/lb; SuperMeat 25,000 L; Gourmey and Aleph at 5,000 L; GOOD Meat 10 x 250,000 L; UPSIDE Glenview to 100,000 L; BlueNalu 8 x 100,000 L; airlifts of 260-300 m3 | Never run in the stated mode |

**Vow** is the single counter-example to stagnation. GFI confirms production at 20,000 L in a 35,000 L facility, the largest operating in the world ([GFI 2026](https://gfi.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/GFI-2026-State-of-the-Industry-report-Cultivated-meat-seafood-and-ingredients.pdf)), and its food-standards dossier describes stirred tanks with complete harvest or draw-and-fill and no cell retention ([FSANZ A1269](https://consultations.foodstandards.gov.au/sas/a1269-cultured-quail-as-a-novel-food/user_uploads/application---cultured-quail-as-a-novel-food.docx.pdf)). The vessel was reportedly built in-house for under $1 million. Its only cost disclosure, cell slurry under $50 per lb in late 2024, comes through a single low-authority outlet ([Lifematetech](https://lifematetech.com/articles/vow-production-scaled-strategy-pivoted)) and sits an order of magnitude above your sterile floor.

**Continuous harvest** rests on two data sets, both with retention and both at bench scale. Pasitka et al. reached 130 x 10^6 cells per mL with repeated harvests over 20 days, then modelled a facility they themselves call theoretical ([Nature Food](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43016-024-01022-w)); the notes could not confirm the run volume and put 80% on single-digit litres. SuperMeat's white paper reports 80 x 10^6 cells per mL in a 10 L reactor and a 45-day bleed ([SuperMeat](https://supermeat.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/A-Deep-Dive-Into-Cost-Effective-Cultivated-Meat-Production-V.pdf)). No peer-reviewed or preprint paper from 2023-2026 reports cultivated-meat culture at 100 L or more with density, doubling time and run length; the notes give 15% to having missed one at 1,000 L or above. No one has published a retention-free continuous run at any scale.

**The failures** all share one shape: steel committed before the process was validated at intermediate scale. Believer completed a plant understood to hold 20,000 L vessels, won FDA and USDA clearance, and closed in December 2025 without shipping a sample; unnamed sources told AgFunderNews that cell growth at scale was unreliable and contamination a concern ([AgFunderNews](https://agfundernews.com/what-went-wrong-at-believer-meats-sources-point-to-risk-scale-and-timing)). That is the only large-scale datapoint on the Pasitka process, and it is negative. GOOD Meat's 250,000 L programme ended in litigation ([AgFunderNews](https://agfundernews.com/breaking-eat-just-good-meat-to-pay-abec-4-4m-to-settle-legal-dispute)), UPSIDE paused Glenview ([Food Processing](https://www.foodprocessing.com/ingredients/alternative-protein/news/33037213/upside-foods-halts-plan-for-new-illinois-cultivated-meat-plant)), and sector funding fell to $73.9 million in 2025 ([GFI 2026](https://gfi.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/GFI-2026-State-of-the-Industry-report-Cultivated-meat-seafood-and-ingredients.pdf)). Fewer funded attempts means the evidence base will grow slowly, which should widen rather than narrow your uncertainty on timing.

## Sterility decides which plant class is reachable

The $5/kg floor is a statement about plant class, and the binding fact is kinetic. Bacteria double in about 20 minutes and animal cells in 24-48 hours, in a warm, neutral, nutrient-rich, antibiotic-free broth, so any ingress converts the culture within about a day ([Humbird report](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/download/1438/2973/2173)). The fermentation plants that cost $70k per m3 are cheap partly because they tolerate low-level contamination; the organism outruns or acid-suppresses invaders. Animal cells cannot, and faster cell lines do not close a 70-140x gap. Quorn's 155 m3 continuous fermenters running about 1,000 hours ([Frontiers 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fsufs.2023.1204307/full)) prove large continuous sterile engineering, not that animal cells can hold a 20 m3 tank for a month.

"Food-grade" currently means two different things. Vow and Meatly mean cheaper steel, in-house fabrication and no classified clean room, while still running a pure culture. The $70k figure belongs to the other meaning, sanitary operation that tolerates foreign organisms, for which the notes found no published or company evidence with animal cells; proposals such as antimicrobial media components remain research wishes ([GFI](https://gfi.org/solutions/cost-sterility-controls-cultivated-meat)). The notes put about **10%** on whole-plant cost reaching $70k per m3 by 2035 and **15-25%** on a public demonstration of 100 m3 or larger animal-cell culture at food-plant conditions with batch loss under 10% by then.

An intermediate class is well supported. Humbird's reference classes put a 20 m3 installed vessel at about $51k per m3 for sanitary (beer), $360k for aseptic (enzyme) and $1.07 million for cell-culture cGMP, so your two tiers skip the aseptic-but-not-pharma rung entirely ([Humbird report](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/download/1438/2973/2173)). Vow's and Meatly's bare-vessel claims of under $50k per m3 imply roughly **$200-300k per m3 installed** at an installation factor of 5-6, and real recent microbial food plants land in the same band: Liberation Labs at about $190k per m3 ([Green Queen](https://www.greenqueen.com.hk/liberation-labs-precision-fermentation-facility-saudi-arabia-funding/)) and Quorn's third fermenter at roughly £190k per m3 ([FoodNavigator](https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2014/04/25/Meat-free-demand-prompts-30M-injection-at-Quorn-Foods/)). Even built microbial plants do not reach the textbook $70k. The notes put **50-60%** on a $200-400k per m3 axenic cultivated-meat plant being demonstrated by 2030. Humbird's dissent deserves weight: he argues installation factors near 7 follow from plant size, not steel grade, and that lower ones are unjustified without detailed design. On your model's numbers a $300k per m3 class costs about **$7.5/kg** at the model's productivity (capital $3.3, labour $1.2, the rest about $3).

On reliability, cultivated-meat data point the wrong way. Surveyed manufacturers lost an average of **11.2%** of batches to contamination, and **19.5%** among the six working beyond flask scale ([Compr. Rev. Food Sci. Food Saf. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11681928/)), against roughly 2% from contamination and 7.6% from all causes in large biopharma facilities ([Humbird report](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/download/1438/2973/2173); [BioProcess International](https://bioprocessintl.com/bioanalytical-methods/biotech-facilities-average-a-batch-failure-every-40-6-weeks)). No dataset reports failure per reactor-day or by run length, so every conversion below is an extrapolation.

## Three changes to the floor model

**Replace fixed uptime with run length, restart time and a failure hazard.** The 0.91 is Humbird's perfusion uptime, about 33-day runs with 72-hour turnarounds and no failures. Converting the batch figures to a constant daily hazard gives about 0.2% per day for biopharma contamination, 0.7% for biopharma all-cause, and 1-2% per day for cultivated meat today if those batches lasted 10-20 days (an assumption; the survey does not say). A 30-day run then ends early with probability 5%, 18% and 28-48% respectively, bracketing the notes' 10-30%. The less obvious result is that in continuous mode the failure itself costs little: product harvested before the event is kept, and the loss is one tank of broth plus an early restart. With a planned 30-day run and seven days to clean and regrow from seed, productive time is 81% with no failures and 78% at 1.5% per day (my arithmetic; assumes independent tanks and prompt detection). The restart overhead does more damage than the failures, and SuperMeat's own model shows the same thing: a 45-day bleed inside a 56.3-day cycle is 80% productive time. Setting uptime to about **0.75-0.80** adds roughly $1.5-2.5/kg to the sterile floor and $0.3-0.4/kg to the fermentation-class floor. Two cases make the term first-order instead: fed-batch tiers, where a 10-20% loss rate scales the whole cost including feed, and plant-wide events such as the single contaminated bioreactor that shut Genzyme's entire Allston site ([GEN](https://www.genengnews.com/topics/bioprocessing/genzyme-temporarily-halts-production-at-plant-in-massachusetts/)), which argue for a small correlated-outage allowance. Runs longer than 20-40 days should not be assumed: even Quorn restarts after about a month because variants take over ([Microbiology Society](https://microbiologysociety.org/resource/mycoprotein-production-and-food-sustainability.html)).

**Derate retention-free productivity and expose it as a parameter.** Capital and labour scale inversely with output, and together they are about $11 of the $14 sterile floor and $2 of the $5 fermentation-class floor. The table shows what follows (my arithmetic from the module's inputs, rounded; the module was not re-run).

| Productivity relative to the model's 22 t/m3/yr | Corresponds to | Sterile plant ($900k/m3) | Intermediate ($300k/m3) | Fermentation class ($70k/m3) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.0 | Model as written | $14/kg | $7.5/kg | $5/kg |
| 0.72 | Fill-and-draw, 50% draws | $18 | $9 | $6 |
| 0.5 | 80% draws, or 48 h doubling | $25 | $12 | $7 |
| 0.29 | Vow's stated target, best reading | $41 | $18 | $10 |

A sensible central case is a factor of 0.5-0.7 with the model's cells, which puts the sterile floor at about **$18-25/kg** before the uptime correction and about $20-30/kg after it. The 120 g/L density should be kept as the "efficient cells" bound and paired with a second case at 50-60 g/L, the level companies claim but have not published.

**Add the intermediate plant class and relabel the cheap one.** A tier at $200-400k per m3, axenic, without clean rooms, in 20-50 m3 vessels, is the only cheap class with operating examples, and it is where the probability mass for 2030 sits. The $70k tier should stay in the ladder as a bound conditional on non-axenic animal-cell culture, a biological breakthrough rather than an engineering extrapolation. Humbird's own cross-check supports the magnitude of that bound: a $57k per m3 yeast plant constrained to animal-cell growth and oxygen limits costs $3.87/kg wet on molasses ([Humbird report](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/download/1438/2973/2173)).

## Conclusion

The question "is continuous harvest at 20-50 m3 reasonable?" has a split answer that the current tier labels blur. As a statement about hardware it is reasonable today. As a statement about biology it is a bet that efficient, fast, waste-frugal cells will hold a pure culture for weeks, and the only organisation that built a plant on a version of that bet failed before validating it. The decisive variables for the floor are therefore not vessel size or steel grade but three numbers no one has published: steady-state density without retention, contamination hazard per reactor-day outside a clean room, and cycles before a forced restart. Vow is the one place those numbers exist, which makes any disclosure from its line, or from co-manufacturing clients such as PARIMA, worth more to the model than another techno-economic study.

The practical reading for the cost model is that its floors are bounds on two different futures. The sterile-plant path leads to roughly $20-25/kg of real cells with engineering that is mostly in hand; the intermediate class leads to perhaps $9-13/kg and is the live frontier; and $5/kg lies beyond a sterility breakthrough the notes rate at about one in ten by 2035. Two leads would tighten this quickly and cheaply: the historical veterinary-vaccine literature on multi-m3 repeated-batch BHK culture, the closest real precedent for fill-and-draw at scale, and the FDA and FSANZ scientific memos, which were not retrieved and may state operating modes and run lengths.
