# Premium animal-product analogues: (price premium, volume share) pairs

Compiled 2026-11-14 from web sources. Purpose: calibration set for the "real meat, equal except price" demand model.

Conventions
- **Premium ratio** = premium price / conventional price. "Derived" means I computed it from two published numbers; the arithmetic is shown.
- **Access**: FT = full text read (PDF or page); SN = search-engine snippet only (not verified against the primary document); AB = abstract/summary page.
- Nothing here is extrapolated. Where share and premium come from different sources or years, the row says so.
- A caveat that applies to every row: none of these products is "identical except price". Each carries a perceived benefit (health, welfare, taste) that should push share UP relative to a pure price penalty, and most carry distribution limits that push share DOWN. Column "Non-price differences" records this.

Note 1 (rows 1-3): in source [S1] the text says "Organic chicken is the largest, at $1.8 billion, just edging out beef, at $1.3 billion" and that beef has the highest growth rate, while the table prints chicken $1.3B / 161.0M lb (+36% $) and beef $1.8B / 297.5M lb (+17% $). Text and table contradict each other. Reading A follows the text (labels swapped in the table) and is internally more plausible: reading B implies organic beef at $6.05/lb, below the all-beef average of $7.14. But reading B gives an organic chicken volume share of 2.5%, close to the 2.4% already used in the model. I could not resolve this from a second source. Organic chicken's volume share is therefore 2.5% or 4.6% of fresh chicken pounds, and the unmatched price ratio 1.9 or 2.6. Neither ratio is cut-matched, so neither contradicts the model's existing 1.44x like-for-like figure.

Reading for the model: premium products are consistently more price-elastic (about -1 to -2, occasionally -3) than their conventional counterparts (-0.2 to -1). The US organic-milk pair is the cleanest: at a size-matched ratio of about 1.63 the product holds about 7% of volume with an own-price elasticity of about -2.

**Share-versus-premium across products**: no published paper fitting share against price premium across premium animal products was found. The nearest thing is the Danish "organic ladder" in [S20], which states qualitatively that share falls as the organic price gap widens (milk and eggs high, chicken and pork low) but gives no premiums.
