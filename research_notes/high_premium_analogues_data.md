# High-premium animal-product analogues (2.5x to 6x): (price premium, share) pairs

Compiled 2026-11-17 from web sources. Purpose: extend the calibration set in `premium_meat_analogues.md` (six products at 1.5-2.2x holding 2-7% of volume) into the 2.5-6x range where cultivated meat is expected to sit. No source already listed in that note is reused here as a primary source; where one is mentioned it is only as a cross-check and is cited by its old [S] number.

Conventions (same as `premium_meat_analogues.md`, plus one column)
- **Premium ratio** = premium price / conventional price. "Derived" means I computed it from two published numbers; the arithmetic is shown.
- **Access**: FT = full text read (PDF or page; for PDFs I extracted the text layer, and for two charts I read the rendered page image); AB = abstract/summary page; SN = search-engine snippet only (not verified against the primary document).
- Nothing here is extrapolated. Where share and price come from different years, periods or sources, the row says so.
- **Supply / distribution limits** is a new column: whether the observed share is capped by supply (land, breed, certification capacity) or by where the product is stocked, rather than by consumer choice at the shelf. It matters because a capped share understates demand at that premium.
- Same caveat as the earlier note: none of these products is "identical except price". Each carries a perceived benefit (welfare, taste, origin, health) that pushes share up relative to a pure price penalty.
- Rows marked "(below band)" are under 2.5x. They are kept only when they come from the same source as a pair in the band, so the source can be compared with itself.

## (c) What the pairs show about share above 2.5x

Above 2.5x, every measured share falls between about 0.15% and 4% of its category, against 2-7% for the 1.5-2.2x set in the earlier note. So share is not flat across premiums. It is roughly two to five times lower in the band. The three within-source comparisons that span the band all point the same way: US eggs (cage-free 2.0x, 8.6% of volume; organic 2.3x, 7.7%; pasture-raised 3.2x, 0.34% mean and 2.15% peak), French chicken (Label Rouge 1.8x, 13% of production; organic 2.7x, 1%), and the UK organic cuts (0.15-3% at 2.3-3.3x). Within the UK cut-matched set there is no clean gradient, though. Legs hold 3% at 2.4x and breast 0.4% at 2.3x, and the extremes (drumsticks 3.3x at 0.15%) are sold by one retailer. Above about 2.3x, distribution and supply seem to matter as much as price. Organic chicken is absent from discounters in the UK, French organic supply is shrinking, Swiss organic demand exceeds supply, and bellota ibérico is capped by dehesa acreage. That last one is why bellota's 9% share of ibérico hams at 2.8-3.4x shows mainly the size of the supply, not shoppers' price sensitivity. Its share of all Spanish pigs is about 1%. The only pair above 3.5x (Denmark, 3.55x, 3.8% of poultry, basis unstated) sits inside the same 0.15-4% range. Taken together, the data fit a small loyal segment that is about 0.5-4% of volume at 2.5-3.5x and is often limited by supply rather than by its willingness to pay. Value shares run two to three times the volume shares. No credible complete pair above 3.6x was found.
