# Low-cost, food-grade routes for animal cell culture (airlift / bubble column / large tanks, sanitary plants, continuous operation) — status as of November 2026

Evidence tags used below: **[DEMO]** = demonstrated practice or measured data; **[MODEL]** = modelling / techno-economic study; **[CLAIM]** = company claim, not independently verified; **[OPINION]** = expert view. "My arithmetic" marks numbers I derived from cited figures (these are in the Inferences sections).

Primary documents read in full text for these notes: Humbird's long report (engrXiv, 2021 version), the Negulescu et al. preprint (engrXiv 2414), Hubbard's 2024 CFD preprint (engrXiv 3788), and the 2025 contamination review in *Compr. Rev. Food Sci. Food Saf.* (PMC11681928). Most plant-cost figures come from trade press and are company claims.

## 1. Airlift and bubble-column reactors for animal cells: largest scale, modelling claims, limits, recent experiments

### Takeaway
Animal cells have been grown commercially in airlifts only at about 1-2 m3 (Celltech/Lonza, low-density hybridoma/CHO batch culture; Humbird cites "up to 10 m3"), and bubble columns have never worked beyond the bench. The 260-300 m3 airlift for animal cells exists only in CFD and TEA models, whose headline results hinge on an oxygen uptake rate that Humbird and a later independent CFD study both say was understated by 5-10x; with corrected uptake, about 100 g/L wet looks marginally feasible in simulation, but nothing above about 20 m3 has been demonstrated for cultivated-meat cells in any reactor type.

### Cited Findings

**Historical demonstrated scale**
- [DEMO] Celltech's industrial monoclonal-antibody process (Lambert, Birch et al., 1987) used airlift production fermenters of 1,000 L working volume; the same paper mentions that a 10,000 L fermenter "has been designed" (designed, not reported as operated) — [Lambert et al. 1987, Tissue Culture Research Communications](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jtca/5/2/5_2_112/_article/-char/en)
- [DEMO] Lonza Biologics (formerly Celltech Biologics) in 1997 described growing CHO, myeloma and hybridoma cells as suspension cultures in batch airlift reactors of 100 to 2,000 L, with a 2,000 L airlift pictured at its UK site — [Chimia 1997](https://chimia.ch/chimia/article/download/1997_290/2172/12827)
- [OPINION/DEMO summary] Humbird: "The airlift reactor with a liquid downcomer has been used for low-density cell culture up to 10 m3, though much smaller reactors of 1-2 m3 are more common. At larger scale, O2 depletion in the ungassed downcomer can become prohibitive" (citing Stanbury, Whitaker & Hall, *Principles of Fermentation Technology*, 2017). He also states that "judicious sparging rules out the bubble-column bioreactor, which has not been successfully used for animal cell culture beyond the bench scale" — [Humbird, Scale-up economics for cultured meat, engrXiv](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/download/1438/2973/2173)
- [DEMO] Largest conventional animal-cell stirred tanks in biopharma are about 20,000 L; single-use up to 6,000 L (as of 2019) — [GFI bioprocess design deep dive](https://gfi.org/deep-dive-cultivated-meat-bioprocess-design/)
- [DEMO, microbial] The largest aerobic fermenter ever built was ICI's 1,500 m3 Pruteen airlift (bacterial single-cell protein); after start-up in 1979 its foaming and sterility-control systems had to be redesigned in 1980, and the plant was decommissioned by 1987. Quorn mycoprotein uses more modest 150-155 m3 airlifts — [Humbird report](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/download/1438/2973/2173); [Moore, 21st Century Guidebook to Fungi, ch. 17.18](https://davidmoore.org.uk/21st_century_guidebook_to_fungi_platinum/Ch17_18.htm)
- [DEMO, microbial] Commercial baker's yeast is typically produced in about 200 m3 bubble columns at oxygen uptake rates of about 150 mol O2/m3/h and a controlled doubling time of 3.5 h — [Humbird report](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/download/1438/2973/2173)

**Modelling claims for 260-300 m3 airlifts**
- [MODEL] Li et al. (Chem. Eng. Sci. 211:115269, Jan 2020; online 2019) designed a conceptual 300 m3, 13.75 m tall airlift for CHO-type cells on microcarriers and concluded by CFD that at least 2 x 10^8 cells/mL could be supported — [Li et al. 2020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0009250919307596); summary in [Negulescu et al. preprint](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/view/2414)
- [OPINION, critique] Humbird's footnote on Li et al.: their simulation showed 46% (3.2 mg/L) dissolved O2 at the top of the sparged riser, about half of which was consumed during a 20-second trip through the downcomer; but "the SOUR [specific O2 uptake rate] assumed in the simulation was only half of what was actually observed in the authors' own reference, and 20% of that predicted" by his stoichiometry; with the correction "available O2 was depleted and ... the cells were stressed at the downcomer exit" — [Humbird report](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/download/1438/2973/2173)
- [MODEL] Independent re-simulation (Hubbard, Upstream Applied Science, engrXiv preprint dated Sept 2024, lattice-Boltzmann CFD at higher resolution, 2 mm bubbles): Li et al.'s density (2.56 x 10^8 cells/mL, which Hubbard equates to about 770 g/L wet) "is unlikely to be viable"; Li's uptake rate was "an order of magnitude lower than the rate used by Humbird". The 100 g/L wet density assumed by Negulescu et al. "is likely to be viable with small modifications [to] geometry or operation"; smaller bubbles (1.5 mm) might allow about 200 g/L. Hubbard notes his analysis covers only reactor performance (O2, CO2 limit of 100 mbar, mixing, hydrodynamic stress), not metabolite inhibition — [Hubbard 2024 preprint](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/view/3788)
- [MODEL] Negulescu et al. (UC Davis; preprint 2022, published Biotechnol. Bioeng. 2023, DOI 10.1002/bit.28324) modelled 100,000 t/yr of unstructured beef in three plant types. Preprint values: 42 m3 stirred tank (STR) COGS $30.4/kg; 210 m3 STR $20.8/kg; 260 m3 airlift (ALR; 260,712 L total, 208,148 L working) $13.0/kg ($11.8/kg without depreciation). CAPEX per facility: $431M (4 kt/yr), $1,158M (20 kt/yr), $352M (25 kt/yr, ALR); each facility has 50 production reactors. Assumptions: 23 h doubling time, 100 g/L wet final density, about $1/L medium, batch time 24-26 days including a 10-day differentiation hold, food-grade 304 stainless steel, steam-in-place, HTST media sterilisation "sized assuming a maximum of 1 contamination per 50 years". ALR price was scaled (0.6 exponent) from a list price of $174,300 for a 190 m3 airlift in a 1997 Sulzer paper; the STR from $300,000 for a 20 m3 food-industry tank. The authors say more CFD and testing are needed "to determine which reactor type is best for mammalian cells at large scales" — [Negulescu et al. preprint](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/view/2414); [PubMed record of published paper](https://www.pubmed.gov/36581609/)
- [MODEL] Negulescu ALR COGS falls below $10/kg only when medium costs drop below about $0.75/L; with zero media cost and a 12 h doubling time the ALR case reaches $2.5/kg — [Negulescu et al. preprint](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/view/2414)
- [MODEL, critique of Negulescu inputs] Hubbard notes Negulescu et al. considered O2 transport but not CO2 transport, mixing time or hydrodynamic stress, and assumed P/V = 500 W/m3 for STRs, an order of magnitude above Humbird's limit — [Hubbard 2024 preprint](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/view/3788)

**What limits large sparged reactors for animal cells**
- [MODEL] Humbird: bioreactors above 20 m3 have lower maximum cell density due to CO2 inhibition, the only remedy being to "sparge harder, possibly to the point of cell death"; he caps superficial gas velocity at 0.006 m/s (0.1 vvm at 20 m3). Modelled maximum densities: about 150 g/L wet in a 20 m3 vessel and about 70 g/L in a 200 m3 vessel — [Humbird 2021, Biotechnol. Bioeng.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8362201); [Humbird report](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/download/1438/2973/2173)
- [MODEL] Humbird's "constrained yeast" illustration: animal-cell-like limits correspond to a maximum oxygen transfer of about 25 mol O2/m3/h versus 150 for yeast bubble columns — [Humbird report](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/download/1438/2973/2173)
- [DEMO/OPINION] Record-high-density animal cell cultures are typically not sparged at all (O2 via headspace); damage from collapsing bubbles "is real" (Nienow 2006) — [Humbird report](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/download/1438/2973/2173)
- [Review] Bubbles under 1 mm can increase shear/cytotoxicity in suspension culture; CO2 stripping gets harder at scale because of surface-to-volume limits; poloxamers mitigate bubble damage — [GFI bioprocess design deep dive](https://gfi.org/deep-dive-cultivated-meat-bioprocess-design/)
- [Review] EU FEASTS project deliverable (citing Ngwa et al. 2025) lists airlifts as low-shear with good oxygen transfer at small scale, but "scale-up is challenging" and foam formation is a risk — [FEASTS D3.4](https://feasts-innovation.eu/deliverables/d-3-4-bioreactor-specifications-for-industrial-production-of-cultured-meat-cultured-seafood/)
- [OPINION] Humbird: "current designs of fermentors larger than ~200 m3 are not practicably sterilized to a level suitable for animal cell culture" (citing Meyer, Minas & Schmidhalter 2017) — [Humbird report](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/download/1438/2973/2173)

**Experimental work 2022-2026**
- [DEMO, lab scale only] A TU Munich group (Engineering in Life Sciences, 2026) built a 3D-printed membrane-aeration module giving bubble-free O2 transfer for lab-scale cell-culture prototyping — [Eng. Life Sci. 10.1002/elsc.70073](https://www.maxapress.com/data/article/els/preview/pdf/10.1002-elsc.70073.pdf)
- [CLAIM] Ark Biotech says it has built a bioreactor with vertical tubes using airlift mixing, and has modelled vessels up to 1,000,000 L; no peer-reviewed performance data — [Freethink](https://www.freethink.com/science/bioreactor-cultivated-meat); [The Spoon](https://thespoon.tech/cultivated-meat-is-on-sale-but-its-pricey-a-new-study-shows-how-to-bring-the-cost-down/)
- [CLAIM] Largest cultivated-meat vessel publicly described as operating: Vow's 20,000 L food-grade stirred reactor (2025) — [AgFunderNews, 10 Apr 2025](https://agfundernews.com/humbird-was-spectacularly-wrong-on-cultivated-meat-economics-says-report-as-vow-predicts-it-will-soon-be-unit-margin-positive)
- [CLAIM, announced only] GOOD Meat announced in 2022 ten 250,000 L bioreactors (cited via Carrington 2022 in Negulescu et al.) — [Negulescu et al. preprint](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/view/2414)

### Inferences
- The demonstrated-to-modelled gap for airlift animal cell culture is roughly 2 m3 (commercial, 1990s) to 260-300 m3 (models): more than two orders of magnitude, with no intermediate pilot data in the public domain. For stirred tanks the gap is about 20 m3 demonstrated to 200+ m3 modelled.
- The modelling literature is converging on "about 100 g/L wet in a ~300 m3 airlift is not ruled out by O2 transfer" (Hubbard) while rejecting Li et al.'s 2 x 10^8 cells/mL. But Hubbard's CO2 handling, shear tolerance and bubble-size assumptions are themselves unvalidated at scale, and none of the models include foaming or contamination risk — the two things that actually sank the Pruteen 1,500 m3 airlift's early operation.
- Negulescu's $13/kg airlift result is driven mainly by cheap vessels (my arithmetic: $352M / (50 x 260.7 m3) = about $27k per m3 of production vessel, versus about $110k/m3 for their 210 m3 STR case and about $207k/m3 for the 42 m3 STR case). It is therefore a "what if food-fermentation capex applied" scenario, not evidence that it applies.
- Bubble-free aeration (membranes, hollow fibres, perfluorocarbons, microbubbles) has no public pilot-scale demonstration for cultivated meat that I could find; it remains lab-scale.

### Gaps
- I could not confirm a 5,000 L Celltech/Lonza airlift from a primary source; the sources found document 1,000 L (1987) and 2,000 L (1997), plus a "designed" 10,000 L vessel and Humbird's secondary "up to 10 m3" statement.
- Published Negulescu et al. 2023 COGS values appear to differ from the preprint (a search summary indicates about $35/kg and $25/kg for the STR cases in the final abstract; airlift value not retrieved). Notes above use preprint numbers; check the published paper before quoting.
- The fate of GOOD Meat's 250,000 L project: my recollection is that it was abandoned amid a dispute with the vendor ABEC in 2023, but I did not retrieve a source in this session.
- No public experimental data found (2022-2026) on airlift, bubble-column, hollow-fibre-oxygenated or perfluorocarbon-assisted culture of cultivated-meat cells at pilot scale (>1 m3). Ark Biotech's hardware claims are unverified.
- Ngwa et al. 2025 (cited by FEASTS) was not read directly.

## 2. Sterility and contamination: why aseptic operation is needed, observed contamination rates, mitigation approaches, and "food-grade" facilities

### Takeaway
The need for asepsis is a kinetic fact, not a regulatory habit: contaminants double in about 20 minutes versus 24-48 hours for animal cells, in a neutral-pH, 37 C, nutrient-rich, antibiotic-free broth, so any ingress ends the batch. Observed contamination losses in cultivated meat today (about 11% of batches on average, about 20% outside flask scale) are several times worse than biopharma, and no one has published evidence of animal cell culture running at fermentation-style ("sanitary", low-level-contamination-tolerant) conditions; what companies call "food-grade" so far means cheaper steel, no classified clean room and simpler vessels, while still running axenically.

### Cited Findings

**Why animal cells need axenic culture and yeast/mycoprotein do not**
- Doubling times: bacteria about 20 min, yeast 1-3 h, mycoplasma 6-12 h, fibroblast-type animal cells 24-48 h (Humbird Table 1.1) — [Humbird report](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/download/1438/2973/2173)
- [OPINION] "At neutral pH and elevated temperature (37 C), an aerated cell-culture bioreactor is quite welcoming to any stray microbe ... a contamination event will very quickly turn an animal cell culture into a microbial culture, leading to batch loss." Industrial commodity fermentation "can generally be carried out in the presence of small numbers of foreign organisms", whereas "any stray microbial life is functionally pathogenic to animal cells in culture" — [Humbird report](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/download/1438/2973/2173)
- [DEMO, industry practice] Truly large fermenters are "reserved for so-called non-sterile fermentations, in which aseptic inoculum is prepared in sterile bioreactors up to ~200 m3 and the final production fermentor is run with a low level of contamination, possibly controlled with antibiotics or chemical agents like chlorine dioxide" — [Humbird report](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/download/1438/2973/2173)
- Antibiotics are generally not used in large-scale upstream cell culture, and cultivated-meat messaging rules them out; most cultivated-meat companies are developing antibiotic-free processes and the products approved so far (UPSIDE, GOOD Meat, Vow dossiers) were made without antibiotics, although antibiotics are commonly used during cell isolation and line derivation — [Humbird report](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/download/1438/2973/2173); [Compr. Rev. Food Sci. Food Saf. 2025;24:e70077](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11681928/)
- Viral contamination and biosafety containment are an additional issue specific to animal cells; Humbird calls the assumption that containment is unnecessary "dubious" — [Humbird report](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/download/1438/2973/2173)

**Observed contamination and batch-failure rates**
- [DEMO, survey] Cultivated meat: targeted survey of manufacturers (May-July 2023; 17 companies, 11 answering this question): microbiological-contamination batch failure rate over the previous 12 months ranged from 0% to 52%, average 11.2%; excluding companies working only at flask (R&D) scale, the average for the remaining six was 19.5%. Most common vectors: personnel, equipment, production environment; most common contaminant: bacteria — [Compr. Rev. Food Sci. Food Saf. 2025;24:e70077 (corresponding authors D. Powell, GFI APAC, and D. Li, NUS)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11681928/)
- [DEMO, survey] GFI's Harsini & Swartz 2024 survey: of six companies with operating pilot/production facilities, all-cause batch failure was 2-5% for three, 5-10% for two, and above 10% for one — [same review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11681928/)
- [DEMO, survey] Biopharma benchmark: about one batch failure per 58 weeks per facility on average (improved from one per 40 weeks in 2008), with contamination accounting for about 3.2% of failures at commercial facilities in 2022 (Ranck 2022, cited in the review) — [same review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11681928/)
- Humbird puts the state-of-the-art biopharma batch failure rate from contamination at "about 2%" (citing Langer's BioPlan surveys) — [Humbird report](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/download/1438/2973/2173)
- [MODEL assumption] Negulescu et al. sized media sterilisation for at most one contamination per 50 years and did not model batch loss; Humbird also did not model batch loss and warns losses "could be expected to increase with ... fewer engineering controls ... or the reduced sterility assurance of lower-grade materials of construction and lower-quality media components" — [Negulescu et al. preprint](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/view/2414); [Humbird report](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/download/1438/2973/2173)
- [DEMO, microbial] Even the 1,500 m3 Pruteen bacterial airlift needed its sterility-control system redesigned after start-up — [Humbird report](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/download/1438/2973/2173)

**Approaches to relax sterility requirements**
- [Proposal] GFI lists as open research needs: intrinsically antimicrobial inputs (certain hydrolysate peptides, plant albumins, chitosan); thermal/irradiation/chlorine-dioxide treatment of inputs and surfaces; more automation and closed systems; image-based contamination detection; and restricting pharma-style clean-room controls to "early pre-production and seed train stages", with later stages run under controls "similar to fermentation-based food production". GFI states it is unknown how far cultivated meat can move away from pharmaceutical practice — [GFI: Reducing the cost of sterility controls](https://gfi.org/solutions/cost-sterility-controls-cultivated-meat)
- [Review] Classified clean rooms "may not be necessary for some, or all, stages" with closed-system equipment; strict gowning may be redundant; a "food-grade" Good Cell Culture Practice has been proposed. Mosa Meat (2021) expected ISO Class 8 for harvesting; UPSIDE's FDA dossier implies no classified clean room for some stages — [Compr. Rev. Food Sci. Food Saf. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11681928/)
- [OPINION] Humbird: de-escalating facility class (clean room to sanitary to outdoors) "assumes that sterility measures are self-contained on the equipment"; this "is already true of most cell culture-grade equipment; the clean room is a risk-reduction measure" justified by pharma regulation and product value. But he judges plastic/lower-grade-steel "commercial kitchen" equipment "probably not consistent with the ultimate sterility requirements of cultured meat" — [Humbird report](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/download/1438/2973/2173)
- [MODEL] In Humbird's fed-batch model, buildings (clean room) contribute only about $3/kg of a $37/kg cost at the optimum of 24 x 20 m3 reactors; halving this (sanitary area) or eliminating it (outdoors) leaves cost above his $25/kg target. Clean-room cost grows faster than process volume and exceeds bioreactor cost at 48 x 20 m3 — [Humbird report](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/download/1438/2973/2173); [Humbird 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8362201)
- [Proposal] Cheaper food-safe single-use bags, pulsed electric field or flash pasteurisation of media are mentioned as options — [GFI bioprocess design deep dive](https://gfi.org/deep-dive-cultivated-meat-bioprocess-design/)

**"Food-grade" / non-pharma cultivated-meat facilities and their cost**
- [CLAIM] Vow (Sydney): in-house designed food-grade 20,000 L reactor, built by ex-SpaceX engineers, commissioned in under 14 weeks for "well under $1 million", described as the largest food-grade cell-culture vessel operating; output up to about 2,200 lb (about 1 t) of cultured quail per month reported; CEO expected unit-margin-positive status in 2025; the company also laid off nearly a third of staff in early 2025 — [AgFunderNews, 10 Apr 2025](https://agfundernews.com/humbird-was-spectacularly-wrong-on-cultivated-meat-economics-says-report-as-vow-predicts-it-will-soon-be-unit-margin-positive); [FoodNavigator, Apr 2025](https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2025/04/10/cultivated-meat-and-fat-inches-toward-retail/)
- [CLAIM] Meatly (UK, pet food): in-house 320 L pilot bioreactor for about £12,500 versus about £250,000 for a comparable biopharma unit (a claimed 95% cut); a 20,000 L pilot facility is planned — [FoodNavigator, 28 May 2025](https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2025/05/28/meatly-cuts-cultivated-meat-costs); [Agronomics/StreetInsider release](https://www.streetinsider.com/Press+Releases/Agronomics+Limited+-+Meatly+Reduces+Bioreactor+and+Medium+Costs/24859852.html)
- [CLAIM] Lever VC white paper (March/April 2025): "second-generation" companies use food-grade bioreactors and tailored processes, "cutting CapEx by an order of magnitude"; cell-mass COGS of $10-15/kg and below $10/kg for some; data under NDA, unpublished — [AgFunderNews, 10 Apr 2025](https://agfundernews.com/humbird-was-spectacularly-wrong-on-cultivated-meat-economics-says-report-as-vow-predicts-it-will-soon-be-unit-margin-positive); [Substack summary](https://webegreen.substack.com/p/humbird-or-humbug-cultivated-meats)
- [CLAIM + outcome] Believer Meats, Wilson NC: cost put at $123M at the December 2022 groundbreaking for a 200,000 sq ft plant; later described as a $150M facility; plant completed and FDA clearance received in 2025, then the company ceased operations in December 2025 with a contractor suing over about $34M unpaid; UPSIDE Foods bid $50M for the assets and withdrew in August 2026 — [Green Queen: Believer shuts down](https://www.greenqueen.com.hk/believer-meats-cultivated-lab-grown-meat-shuts-down-lawsuit/); [AgFunderNews: what went wrong at Believer](https://agfundernews.com/what-went-wrong-at-believer-meats-sources-point-to-risk-scale-and-timing); [AgFunderNews: UPSIDE drops bid](https://agfundernews.com/breaking-upside-foods-drops-bid-for-believer-meats-us-cultivated-meat-facility)
- [GFI estimate] Food-grade 304 stainless 42 m3 stirred tank about $902k versus $1.5M in pharma-grade 316L (2022 GFI presentation) — [GFI cost-curve slides (E. Swartz)](https://www.slideshare.net/ElliotSwartz/cultivated-meat-cost-curvepdf)

### Inferences
- Growth-rate ratio (my arithmetic from Humbird Table 1.1): 24-48 h / 20 min = 72-144x in doubling rate. A single viable bacterium entering a 100 m3 batch outgrows 10^8 cells/mL of animal cells within roughly a day. This is why "tolerating low-level contamination", the actual practice that makes 200-1,500 m3 food/feed fermenters cheap, is unavailable; yeast and Fusarium rely on speed, low pH and (for Quorn) continuous washout, none of which transfer.
- Two different things are being called "food-grade": (a) cheaper hardware and no clean room while still axenic (Vow, Meatly, Negulescu's assumption); and (b) genuinely fermentation-class sanitary operation. Only (a) has any demonstrated examples, at up to 20 m3. The user's $70k/m3 "food-fermentation-class" figure corresponds to (b)-class plants in the microbial world; whether (a) alone gets there is unproven.
- Current data point the wrong way on reliability: contamination loss is higher, not lower, outside the lab (19.5% vs 11.2%), and 2-10+% all-cause failure even at well-run pilots. A cost model for the cheap route should carry an explicit batch-loss term; for continuous/perfusion runs of 20+ days a contamination event costs proportionally more.
- Long continuous runs raise the stakes on sterility (more connections, more media throughput, longer exposure), so "food-grade" and "continuous" pull against each other unless equipment-level closure is excellent.

### Gaps
- No published contamination statistics for large food fermentations (yeast, Quorn, lysine) were retrieved for a like-for-like comparison; Humbird and Meyer et al. describe the practice qualitatively.
- No published or company evidence found of antimicrobial peptides, engineered contamination-resistant lines, low-pH/thermophilic/psychrophilic operation, or selective media actually enabling non-axenic animal cell culture. These remain proposals (GFI) rather than results.
- Capital cost of the Believer plant per m3 of bioreactor could not be computed: installed vessel volume was not found. The 10,000 t/yr claimed capacity in the brief was not re-verified in this session.
- No cost figures found for The Cultivated B's bioreactors. Ark Biotech's capex-per-litre figures were not retrieved (the full "Path to Price Parity" PDF was not accessed).
- Vow and Meatly vessel prices are bare-vessel company claims; installed-plant cost per m3 is unknown.

## 3. Capital cost evidence: built/announced food and feed fermentation plants versus cultivated-meat TEA assumptions

### Takeaway
Engineering references put a roughly 15x spread on vessel price and a further 2x on installation factors between a lysine-class fermenter and a cGMP cell-culture bioreactor, which reproduces the brief's order of magnitude ($70k vs $900k per m3). But Humbird's key point is that capex per m3 is not the binding variable: a yeast plant built at about $57k/m3 but run at animal-cell growth rate and oxygen-transfer limits makes one-tenth the product and costs about $3.9/kg wet before any animal-cell media costs. Real precision-fermentation and gas-fermentation plants built in 2022-2026 came in at roughly $150-250k per m3 of fermenter, well above textbook commodity numbers.

### Cited Findings

**Engineering cost references by facility class**
- [MODEL/reference] Humbird Table 3.1 (after Petrides/SuperPro): overall Lang factor (total capital / purchased equipment) of 4.7 for non-sterile outdoor (lysine), 6.1 sanitary indoor (beer), 7.1 aseptic indoor (enzyme), 8.8 cGMP microbial (insulin), 10.9 cGMP cell culture (mAb). Typical final vessel: 500 m3 at $747k (lysine); 135 m3 at $470k (beer); 90 m3 at $1.59M (aseptic); 50 m3 at $1.30M (cGMP microbial); 20 m3 at $1.96M (mAb). Cost of a 20 m3 vessel by class: $133k, $168k, $1.01M, $1.01M, $1.96M (a "15x variation") — [Humbird report](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/download/1438/2973/2173)
- Building costs (2018$) per m2 per level: sanitary $1,980; ISO Class 8 clean room $3,760; Class 7 $4,990; Class 6 $8,750 — [Humbird report](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/download/1438/2973/2173)
- [MODEL] Humbird's own cultured-meat estimates: effective Lang factor 7.2 (fed-batch) and 6.9 (perfusion); 20 m3 bioreactor $330k bare, $1.5M installed; installed costs of $2.6M (50 m3), $4.0M (100 m3), $6.8M (200 m3); fed-batch plant of 24 x 20 m3 (649 m3 total bioreactor volume incl. seed) with total capital investment $328M producing 6.8 kt/yr wet cell mass at $37/kg; perfusion plant 96 x 2 m3, $663M, $51/kg. Includes a clean room of about $110M. Capital charge 15%/yr — [Humbird 2021, Biotechnol. Bioeng. 118:3239](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8362201); [Humbird report](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/download/1438/2973/2173)
- [MODEL] Humbird's baker's yeast benchmark: 6 x 200 m3 bubble columns at $818k each; total purchased equipment $15.3M; Lang 4.5; total capital $68.7M; 17.3 kt/yr dry yeast; cost $1.89/kg dry ($0.57/kg wet), consistent with wholesale prices of $2.20-2.80/kg dry — [Humbird report](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/download/1438/2973/2173)
- [MODEL] Same capital ($70M) re-configured under animal-cell-like constraints (growth rate 0.03/h, oxygen transfer 25 mol/m3/h, 27 x 20 m3 vessels, 25 g/L dry): output falls to 1.7 kt/yr and cost rises to $12.90/kg dry ($3.87/kg wet), of which capital charge $4.74, overhead $3.63, labour $3.34 per kg dry — still on cheap molasses/ammonia medium — [Humbird report](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/download/1438/2973/2173)
- [OPINION] Humbird: effective Lang factors of about 7 are "very much a function of facility size and the general size of the equipment ... regardless of its materials of construction ... Without more detailed design, a lower Lang factor is not justified"; and "Capital cost reduction is a secondary condition at best" (media dominates) — [Humbird report](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/download/1438/2973/2173); [Humbird 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8362201)

- [MODEL] CE Delft 2021 (for GFI/GAIA): hypothetical 2030 facility, 10 kt/yr, each line a 10,000 L stirred tank feeding four 2,000 L perfusion reactors; capital about $450M baseline, about $320M with improved efficiency; lowest-cost scenario $5.66/kg (assumes food-grade inputs and long-payback "social" financing). - [MODEL] Negulescu et al.: food-grade pricing; CAPEX per facility $431M (42 m3 STR, 4 kt/yr), $1,158M (210 m3 STR, 20 kt/yr), $352M (260 m3 ALR, 25 kt/yr) — [Negulescu et al. preprint](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/view/2414)
- [GFI estimate] Stirred-tank cultivated-meat facilities "could cost anything from $300 million to well over $1 billion", capital $45k-$109k per annual ton — [AgFunderNews on GFI analysis](https://agfundernews.com/gfi-highlights-chicken-and-egg-conundrum-facing-cultivated-meat-industry)

- [MODEL] Pasitka et al. 2024 (Nature Food; partly Believer-funded): hypothetical 50,000 L continuous (TFF perfusion) facility producing 2.14 kt/yr of cultivated chicken at $6.2/lb (about $13.7/kg), with $0.63/L animal-component-free medium — [Green Queen](https://www.greenqueen.com.hk/believer-meats-cultivated-chicken-lab-grown-meat-cost-study/); [HUJI record](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/empirical-economic-analysis-shows-cost-effective-continuous-manuf-2/)
- [MODEL] GFI, "Optimizing cultivated meat techno-economics: cell growth modeling review and recommendations" (Frohlich, Harsini, Swartz; late 2025): finds cell density, media cost and facility utilisation the decisive levers; density gains beat marginal media-price cuts; warns that overestimated cell mass (wet-mass-per-cell assumptions) artificially lowers $/kg in earlier TEAs — [GFI report page](https://gfi.org/cm-tea-report); [Protein Production Technology summary](https://proteinproductiontechnology.com/post/new-techno-economic-analysis-finds-cell-density-and-accurate-biomass-modeling-key-to-cutting-cultivated-meat-costs)

### Inferences
(All "my arithmetic"; denominators are final production-vessel volume unless stated, so they overstate $/m3 relative to total bioreactor volume.)
- Humbird reference classes, installed cost per m3 of a 20 m3 vessel = vessel price x Lang factor / 20: lysine class about $31k/m3; beer/sanitary about $51k/m3; aseptic about $360k/m3; cGMP microbial about $445k/m3; cGMP cell culture about $1.07M/m3. At each class's typical final-vessel size: lysine 500 m3 about $7k/m3; beer 135 m3 about $21k/m3; mAb 20 m3 about $1.07M/m3. The brief's $900k/m3 (pharma) and $70k/m3 (food fermentation) sit squarely within these reference classes, i.e. the 13x ratio is defensible as a class difference.
- Whole-plant TEA figures per m3 of production vessel: Humbird fed-batch $328M / 480 m3 = about $680k/m3 (about $505k/m3 on all 649 m3 of bioreactors); Humbird yeast plant $68.7M / 1,200 m3 = about $57k/m3; Negulescu 42 m3 STR about $207k/m3, 210 m3 STR about $110k/m3, 260 m3 ALR about $27k/m3.
- Per annual tonne of wet product: Humbird fed-batch about $48k/t; CE Delft about $32-45k/t; GFI range $45-109k/t; Negulescu ALR about $14k/t, 210 m3 STR about $58k/t, 42 m3 STR about $108k/t; Believer's claim ($123M for the brief's 10,000 t/yr) about $12k/t, which would be 3-9x below every independent estimate and was never demonstrated before the company failed.
- Real recent microbial plants are not at $70k/m3: Liberation Labs about $190k/m3 ($115M / 600 m3, incl. downstream); Quorn's third fermenter about £190k/m3 if the £30M bought one 155 m3 fermenter with ancillaries (about £2k per annual tonne of mycoprotein thanks to continuous high-rate culture). Solar Foods Factory 01 is about €220k per annual tonne at demo scale. Greenfield, first-of-a-kind, aseptic-microbial food plants in the US/EU in the 2020s cost roughly 3x the textbook commodity figure; $70k/m3 looks achievable only for nth-of-a-kind, large-vessel (200+ m3), sanitary-class builds.
- Bare-vessel claims from "food-grade" cultivated meat: Vow under $50k/m3 (under $1M / 20 m3); Meatly about £39k/m3 (£12.5k / 0.32 m3). With a Lang factor of about 5-6 these would imply roughly $200-300k/m3 installed, between the two classes in the brief. GFI's 304-vs-316L comparison gives only about a 40% vessel saving, so steel grade alone explains little; the big deltas are skid customisation, validation/documentation, clean rooms and vendor margins.
- Humbird's constrained-yeast case is the cleanest test of the brief's cheap route: at about $57k/m3 and animal-like kinetics, capital + overhead + labour alone give about $3.5/kg wet (of the $3.87 total), before amino-acid media, growth factors, perfusion hardware or batch losses. That is consistent with the brief's about $5/kg floor being roughly the right magnitude if (and only if) fermentation-class plant cost and reliability are achieved at animal-cell kinetics.

### Gaps
- Capital costs not found: Calysseo Chongqing, Unibio/Gulf Biotech Qatar, Perfect Day/large precision-fermentation plants, Angel Yeast plants, lysine plants (CJ, ADM, Evonik). Only Humbird's SuperPro-derived lysine/beer reference numbers were obtained.
- Quorn: whether the £30M "third fermenter" was a single 155 m3 vessel and what ancillaries were included is unclear from trade press; treat the per-m3 figure as rough.
- Solar Foods fermenter volumes (I recall about 20 m3 at Factory 01) were not confirmed, so no $/m3 computed.
- GFI's late-2025 TEA review: specific capex and $/kg numbers were not retrieved; only secondary summaries were read.
- CE Delft's food-grade capex factors and financing ("social investment criteria") were not read from the primary report in this session.
- Garrison et al. 2022 was not retrieved.

## 4. Insect and fish cells as easier alternatives

### Takeaway
Insect cells are the most credible "easier physics" option on paper (room-temperature growth, no CO2 buffering need claimed, serum-free suspension culture, long biopharma track record with Sf9), and a preliminary TEA suggests lower cost, but evidence for food use is at shake-flask scale (over 2 x 10^7 cells/mL) and the cost analysis is a re-parameterised calculator, not process data. Fish cells run cooler but generally grow more slowly, and Humbird argues there is no reason to expect better economics; neither removes the need for axenic culture.

### Cited Findings
- [MODEL] Ashizawa, Rubio, Letcher, Parkinson, Dmitruczyk & Kaplan (Foods 2022, 11:3037) adapted the UC Davis cost calculator to three insect lines (Sf-9, High Five, S2) and concluded insect cells "offer potential to significantly reduce the cost per kilogram", on the hypothesis that they are "more resilient in cell culture than traditional livestock-derived cells". Reviewers noted the paper takes for granted that simple insect biomass is an adequate meat replacement — [Ashizawa et al. 2022](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9564176/); [MDPI review report](https://www.mdpi.com/2304-8158/11/19/3037/review_report)
- [DEMO, lab] Letcher et al. (Scientific Reports, March 2025; Tufts/Kaplan lab) established a non-adherent *Manduca sexta* embryonic line adapted to single-cell suspension in animal-free medium, reaching over 20 million cells/mL in shake flasks — [Sci. Rep. 2025 record](https://doaj.org/article/13cd75a63a6942aab093da7d4de596d5)
- [Claimed advantages] Insect cells can be grown near room temperature (lower energy), adapt to serum-free media and often grow in free suspension, enabling "cost-effective, high-density cell generation" — [New Harvest: insect cell culture project](https://new-harvest.org/research-projects/insect-cell-culture); [Rubio et al. 2019, Front. Sustain. Food Syst.](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sustainable-food-systems/articles/10.3389/fsufs.2019.00024/epub)
- [DEMO, lab] Fish: Saad et al. (Sci. Rep. 2023) established a spontaneously immortalised Atlantic mackerel muscle line ("Mack"); GFI's cell-line guide lists it at 27 C with doubling time falling from 64 h (primary) to 24 h after spontaneous immortalisation, and notes fish cell doubling times "tend to be long compared to mammalian cell types" — [Saad et al. 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-31822-2.pdf); [GFI cultivated seafood cell lines guide](https://gfi.org/cultivated-seafood-cell-lines-guide)
- O2 solubility is higher at lower temperature, so fish cultures may face a somewhat different oxygen-transfer problem — [GFI bioprocess design deep dive](https://gfi.org/deep-dive-cultivated-meat-bioprocess-design/)
- [OPINION] Humbird: growth rate falls with temperature ("If cells are not metabolizing, then they are not growing"), a reduced growth rate requires more bioreactors, and "it is not clear that the economics of cultured seafood should be any different from those presented here" — [Humbird report](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/download/1438/2973/2173)

### Inferences
- Lower culture temperature (27-28 C for insect, 15-28 C for fish) reduces cooling load and raises O2 solubility but does not change the contamination kinetics much: many environmental bacteria and fungi still double in well under an hour at 25-28 C, so the growth-rate ratio that forces asepsis remains of order 30-100x.
- The most transferable insect-cell advantage for a cheap plant is probably robustness in suspension and tolerance of sparging/high osmolality (decades of Sf9/baculovirus manufacturing in stirred tanks), plus spontaneous immortality; but the product is insect biomass, with unproven consumer and regulatory acceptance as "meat".
- For a cost model, insect cells are a plausible scenario for lower media and energy costs, not for a different facility class.

### Gaps
- No pilot-scale (>100 L) food-oriented insect or fish cell culture data found; no measured costs.
- No source retrieved confirming CO2-free/bicarbonate-free operation for food-relevant insect lines, maximum densities in bioreactors (biopharma Sf9 literature not searched), or contamination tolerance.
- Specific Ashizawa $/kg outputs were not extracted (only the abstract-level conclusion).
- Evidence that fish/marine cells tolerate hypoxia better comes from a single low-quality review surfaced in search and was not verified; omitted from findings.

## 5. Cell-line directions that would change the physics

### Takeaway
Reported progress is real but incremental relative to what the cheap route needs: about 24 h doubling times remain the norm; self-sufficient (autocrine growth factor) lines exist but grow more slowly; the highest published density for a food cell line is about 1.3 x 10^8 cells/mL in perfusion at bench scale, reported by a company that has since closed. No peer-reviewed report was found of a food-relevant animal cell line with a doubling time well under 20 h, and densities above 100 g/L are published only for perfusion (not fed-batch) and with contested wet-mass accounting.

### Cited Findings
- [MODEL baseline] Humbird assumes a maximum growth rate of 0.029/h (24 h doubling); wild-type metabolism gives lactate/glucose 1.77 and limits fed-batch density through lactate/ammonia; an "enhanced" hypothetical metabolism (lactate/glucose 0.50) reaches about 110 g/L wet in fed-batch before ammonia inhibition and about 195 g/L in perfusion at 1 reactor volume/day; viscosity caps density near 258 g/L wet (cell volume fraction about 0.25) — [Humbird 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8362201)
- [DEMO, bench + MODEL] Pasitka et al. (Nature Food, Aug 2024): spontaneously immortalised chicken fibroblasts in tangential-flow-filtration perfusion reached 130 x 10^6 cells/mL, reported as 43% weight/volume, over more than 20 days with repeated harvests, in animal-component-free medium costing $0.63/L. The cost figure ($6.2/lb) is for a hypothetical 50,000 L facility. Study partly funded by Believer Meats — [Green Queen](https://www.greenqueen.com.hk/believer-meats-cultivated-chicken-lab-grown-meat-cost-study/); [HUJI record](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/empirical-economic-analysis-shows-cost-effective-continuous-manuf-2/); [FoodNavigator 2024](https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2024/09/03/Breakthrough-in-cultivated-meat-can-lower-production-costs)
- [DEMO, lab] Stout et al. (Tufts; Cell Reports Sustainability, Jan 2024): immortalised bovine satellite cells engineered to express FGF2 and/or RasG12V proliferated over multiple passages without added FGF2, but with slower growth and reduced myotube formation; authors say not ready for commercial use — [FoodNavigator, 31 Jan 2024](https://foodnavigator.com/Article/2024/01/31/cultivated-meat-cells-engineered-to-produce-growth-factors); [paper record](https://doaj.org/article/7db4b25dd4fd4cd982ba5c70b1f80d7f)
- [CLAIM] Lever VC (2025): several companies reach 60-90 g/L (versus what Lever describes as Humbird's 60 g/L ceiling for air-sparged fed-batch); media at or below $1/L for many and below $0.50/L for several; spontaneous immortalisation, suspension culture and low-growth-factor media "massively reduce opex and capex". Data under NDA. Vow says it uses low concentrations of two growth factors and no albumin — [AgFunderNews, 10 Apr 2025](https://agfundernews.com/humbird-was-spectacularly-wrong-on-cultivated-meat-economics-says-report-as-vow-predicts-it-will-soon-be-unit-margin-positive)
- [MODEL] Sensitivity to growth rate is modest when media is expensive: in Negulescu's 260 m3 airlift case at $4/L medium, cutting doubling time six-fold from 72 h to 12 h lowers COGS by only about $5.5/kg, while halving media cost saves about $20/kg — [Negulescu et al. preprint](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/view/2414)
- [MODEL] GFI's late-2025 review finds raising viable cell density gives larger cost gains than marginal media-price cuts, and warns that over-estimated per-cell mass inflates apparent g/L and deflates $/kg — [Protein Production Technology summary](https://proteinproductiontechnology.com/post/new-techno-economic-analysis-finds-cell-density-and-accurate-biomass-modeling-key-to-cutting-cultivated-meat-costs); [GFI report page](https://gfi.org/cm-tea-report)
- [DEMO, lab] Spontaneous immortalisation shortened a fish muscle line's doubling time from 64 h to 24 h — [GFI cultivated seafood cell lines guide](https://gfi.org/cultivated-seafood-cell-lines-guide)

### Inferences
- The Pasitka "43% w/v" at 1.3 x 10^8 cells/mL implies about 3.3 ng wet mass per cell, roughly double Humbird's 1.5 ng fibroblast (Table 1.1) and above his 258 g/L viscosity cap; this is exactly the kind of cell-mass accounting the GFI 2025 review flags. Treat mass-based density claims above about 200-250 g/L with caution; cell counts are the safer anchor.
- What would actually shift the facility class is not density or growth factors but closing the 70-140x growth-rate gap to contaminants or making the culture selective. Even an 8-12 h doubling time (not reported for any food line) leaves a 25-35x gap, so faster lines reduce capex per kg roughly linearly but do not unlock non-axenic operation.
- Growth rate matters most in the cheap-media regime the brief is exploring: once media is under about $1/L, capital and overhead dominate and productivity (growth rate x density x uptime) becomes the main lever, which is the regime of Humbird's constrained-yeast case.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed report found of food-relevant animal cells with doubling times under 20 h in suspension at scale, or of fed-batch densities above 100 g/L wet; company claims of 60-90 g/L are unpublished.
- Lactate-/ammonia-efficient engineered food lines (e.g. glutamine-synthetase or LDH-modified) were not searched specifically; no results to report.
- Long-run genetic stability of continuous cultures (drift over 20+ days) is flagged by Humbird as an open issue; no food-cell data found.

## 6. Expert views on plausibility and timescale

### Takeaway
Independent bioprocess engineers (Humbird, Wood) regard fermentation-style animal cell culture as unproven and, on present evidence, unlikely; sector scientists (GFI) agree pharma-grade sterility is incompatible with food economics and treat how far it can be relaxed as an open research question; investors and companies (Lever VC, Vow, Meatly) claim an order-of-magnitude capex cut has already been achieved at up to 20 m3, with data unpublished. Nobody credible offers a dated forecast for 100 m3+ food-grade animal cell culture, and two flagship scale-up attempts (Believer; GOOD Meat's 250 m3 plan) did not reach operation.

### Cited Findings
- [OPINION] Humbird (2021 conclusion): animal cell cultures are "highly susceptible to microbial contamination, but ... the costs of pharma-grade engineering and equipment would be inconsistent with the food-scale" economics; metabolic enhancement and cheap media are "necessary but insufficient conditions of affordability"; he proposes about $25/kg wet as a realistic target and finds $37/kg (fed-batch) and $51/kg (perfusion), or about $22/kg with hydrolysate media — [Humbird 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8362201); [Humbird report](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/download/1438/2973/2173)
- [OPINION] Humbird lists as future work whether cell culture could run "in significantly larger bioreactors, perhaps located in a less controlled environment like a warehouse or indeed a brewery", i.e. he treats it as an open, simulation- and experiment-testable question rather than impossible — [Humbird report](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/download/1438/2973/2173)
- [OPINION] Humbird, April 2025, responding to Lever VC: company claims exist "only in press releases, not peer-reviewed work"; $/L media comparisons are meaningless without litres per kg of cells; calls the report a "tacky ad hom on a five-year-old paper" with "a few quantitative misrepresentations" — [AgFunderNews, 10 Apr 2025](https://agfundernews.com/humbird-was-spectacularly-wrong-on-cultivated-meat-economics-says-report-as-vow-predicts-it-will-soon-be-unit-margin-positive)
- [OPINION] Paul Wood (ex-Pfizer Animal Health, vaccine manufacturing): "sterility is like pregnancy, you can't be a little bit sterile"; a cultivated-meat plant "looks like a pharmaceutical factory and is expensive"; claims about 250,000 L vessels are "virtue signalling to investors"; costs need to fall 100- to 1,000-fold (statements from 2021-2022) — [FoodNavigator, June 2022](https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2022/06/22/Clean-cell-cultured-meat-unviable-alternative-for-now-Prof/); [The Counter, 2021](https://thecounter.org/lab-grown-cultivated-meat-cost-at-scale/); [Beef Central](https://www.beefcentral.com/news/cell-based-meat-is-facing-a-tougher-road-than-startups-are-letting-on/)
- [OPINION] GFI: the cost of biopharma-style sterility "is incongruent with large-scale food production"; ideal sterility practices could "limit the bioreactor size"; "further research is needed to determine the degree to which" cultivated meat should depart from pharma practice — [GFI: sterility controls](https://gfi.org/solutions/cost-sterility-controls-cultivated-meat)
- [OPINION] Elliot Swartz (GFI principal scientist), April 2025: has become "more optimistic" about cultivated-meat techno-economics since Humbird's analysis, but could not comment on Lever's numbers — [AgFunderNews, 10 Apr 2025](https://agfundernews.com/humbird-was-spectacularly-wrong-on-cultivated-meat-economics-says-report-as-vow-predicts-it-will-soon-be-unit-margin-positive)
- [OPINION, review] 2025 food-safety review (GFI/NUS authors): the industry "is yet to realize the microbiological safety potential" of the technology; contamination issues "should be surmountable" as HACCP/GMP/GCCP systems mature; classified clean rooms may be unnecessary with closed systems — [Compr. Rev. Food Sci. Food Saf. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11681928/)
- [OPINION] Rethink Priorities' comparison of TEAs summarises Humbird's position that any bacterial or viral contamination in an animal cell reactor is fatal to the batch and that mass-transfer limits apply in large reactors — [Rethink Priorities](https://rethinkpriorities.org/publications/cultured-meat-a-comparison-of-techno-economic-analyses)
- [OPINION, academic] UC Davis (Negulescu et al.): large airlifts could reach about $13/kg but "more studies need to be performed and tested" to establish which reactor type suits mammalian cells at scale — [Negulescu et al. preprint](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/view/2414)
- [Market signal] Believer Meats completed a (claimed) low-cost large plant and shut down in December 2025; cultivated meat and seafood funding fell to $73.9M in 2025 from $139M in 2024 (GFI figures via secondary source) — [Green Queen](https://www.greenqueen.com.hk/believer-meats-cultivated-lab-grown-meat-shuts-down-lawsuit/); [Substack summary citing GFI](https://webegreen.substack.com/p/humbird-or-humbug-cultivated-meats)
- [Projection] CE Delft/GFI 2021 framed $5.66/kg as achievable by 2030 in a best-case scenario; no later independent source reaffirms that date — [GFI blog](https://gfi.org/blog/cultivated-meat-lca-tea/)

### Inferences
- Positions line up by evidence standard rather than by fundamental disagreement about physics: sceptics cite the absence of published data above 20 m3 and the contamination kinetics; optimists cite unpublished company data at up to 20 m3 in cheaper hardware. Both are compatible with "cheaper-than-pharma axenic plants are real at 20 m3; fermentation-class 100+ m3 plants are undemonstrated".
- My calibrated read (not sourced; for the report writer to treat as analyst judgement): probability that by 2035 someone has publicly demonstrated sustained animal cell culture in a vessel of 100 m3 or more at food-plant (non-clean-room) conditions with batch loss under 10%: roughly 15-25%. Probability that installed whole-plant cost for cultivated meat reaches about $70k/m3 at any scale by 2035: roughly 10%. Probability that $200-400k/m3 (axenic but de-pharma'd, 20-50 m3 vessels) is demonstrated by 2030: roughly 50-60%, since bare-vessel claims already imply it. Main drivers of uncertainty: funding collapse (fewer attempts), and lack of published reliability data.
- For the cost model: treat $900k/m3 and $70k/m3 as bracketing cases, with a central "de-pharma'd axenic" case around $200-400k/m3, and add an explicit batch-failure term (2-5% optimistic, 10-20% current reality outside flasks). On the brief's own numbers ($14/kg at $900k/m3; $5/kg at $70k/m3), linear interpolation in capex puts a $300k/m3 plant at about $7.5/kg before batch-loss adjustment (my arithmetic; assumes capital-linked cost scales linearly with $/m3, which the brief's two points imply at about $1.08/kg per $100k/m3).
